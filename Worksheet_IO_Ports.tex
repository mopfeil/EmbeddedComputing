\section*{Overview}

Every GPIO pin ends in an output stage: two transistors (push-pull) or one transistor to ground (open drain,
open collector). In this worksheet you will take these stages apart in a circuit simulator. You will find out
how much current a pin can deliver, how fast it can switch a load, why a pull-up resistor makes a bus slow,
and which voltages and currents destroy a pin or the circuit behind it.

\textbf{Learning outcomes.} After this worksheet you can
\begin{itemize}
  \item model a GPIO output stage by on-resistance and saturation current and relate the model to the
        $V_{OH}$/$V_{OL}$ specifications of a data sheet,
  \item predict rise and fall times, the maximum toggle frequency and the dynamic power of a push-pull output,
  \item dimension the pull-up resistor of an open-drain bus such as I$^2$C from rise-time and $V_{OL}$ limits,
  \item judge where the maximum output voltage and current of a pin come from (body diodes, injection current,
        back-powering, transistor breakdown and saturation) and protect a driver against inductive kick-back,
  \item recognise when a PCB trace has to be treated as a transmission line and terminate it.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Organisation.} About 4 hours, in teams of two. You do not have to calculate: all results of the
model are given in tables. Tasks marked \textsf{[P]} (preparation) ask you to read these values and understand
where they come from \emph{before} you open the simulation. \textsf{[S]} means simulate, measure and enter your
values in the empty column \emph{Simulation} of the tables. \textsf{[D]} means discuss and explain. Hand in a short report (PDF): for each task the completed tables, screenshots of the
scopes and your explanation. When a measurement differs from the model value by
more than about \SI{10}{\percent}, explain why.

\textbf{Tool.} Falstad CircuitJS runs in the browser: \url{https://www.falstad.com/circuit/}. Each part of this
sheet has a link that opens the prepared circuit. The circuit files are also in the folder
\href{https://github.com/mopfeil/EmbeddedComputing/tree/main/examples/interfaces/falstad}{\texttt{examples/interfaces/falstad/}}
of the lecture repository (open them with \emph{File $\rightarrow$ Open File}).

\begin{modelbox}{The transistor model used in all circuits}
The MOSFETs follow the square-law (Shichman--Hodges) model with threshold $V_T=\SI{0.6}{V}$ and
\[
  I_D=\begin{cases}
     \beta\left[(V_{GS}-V_T)\,V_{DS}-\tfrac12 V_{DS}^2\right] & V_{DS} < V_{GS}-V_T \quad\text{(triode)}\\[1mm]
     \tfrac{\beta}{2}\,(V_{GS}-V_T)^2 & V_{DS} \ge V_{GS}-V_T \quad\text{(saturation)}
  \end{cases}
\]
with $\beta_N=\SI{12}{mA/V^2}$ for the NMOS and $\beta_P=\SI{8}{mA/V^2}$ for the PMOS of the output stage (PMOS:
use $V_{SG}$, $V_{SD}$). Both have a body diode. The supply is $V_{DD}=\SI{3.3}{V}$.
The model knows no gate or junction capacitances, no temperature and no breakdown. Pin and load capacitances are
drawn as explicit capacitors. Keep in mind what the model cannot show when you interpret the results.
\end{modelbox}

\section{Push-pull output: static characteristic}
%======================================================================
\circuitlink{\linkA}{A\_pushpull\_characteristic.txt}

The logic input is the bit in the output register. Two inverters (the predriver and the CMOS stage) make the pin
follow it. A triangle voltage source acts as a curve tracer: it forces the pin voltage from \SI{-0.5}{V} to
\SI{3.8}{V}, and an ammeter measures the pin current (positive = current out of the pin). The left scope shows the
current over the voltage, the right scope the power in both transistors.

\schematic[0.55]{A}

\task{P} Table~1.1 gives the on-resistance (valid for small $V_{DS}$) and the saturation current of both
transistors for $V_{GS}=V_{DD}$. Which one is the stronger transistor, and why do chip designers make the PMOS
larger than the NMOS?

\begin{results}{Table 1.1: model values of the output transistors ($V_{GS}=\SI{3.3}{V}$)}{lccc}
  & formula & NMOS & PMOS\\ \midrule
  $\beta$ & & \SI{12}{mA/V^2} & \SI{8}{mA/V^2}\\
  $R_{on}$ & $1/(\beta(V_{GS}-V_T))$ & \SI{30.9}{\ohm} & \SI{46.3}{\ohm}\\
  $I_{sat}$ & $\tfrac{\beta}{2}(V_{GS}-V_T)^2$ & \SI{43.7}{mA} & \SI{29.2}{mA}\\
\end{results}

\begin{solution}
$R_{on,N}=1/(\SI{12}{mA/V^2}\cdot\SI{2.7}{V})=\SI{30.9}{\ohm}$, $R_{on,P}=1/(\SI{8}{mA/V^2}\cdot\SI{2.7}{V})=\SI{46.3}{\ohm}$.
$I_{sat,N}=\tfrac12\cdot 12\cdot 2.7^2\,\si{mA}=\SI{43.7}{mA}$, $I_{sat,P}=\SI{29.2}{mA}$.
The NMOS is stronger. Hole mobility is about 2--3 times lower than electron mobility, so a PMOS needs a
correspondingly wider channel for the same current. Here the PMOS is still weaker, which is typical: data sheets
often allow a larger $V_{OH}$ drop than $V_{OL}$ rise, or specify asymmetric currents.
\end{solution}

Also read the short-circuit currents (pin at \SI{0}{V} for \textsf{H}, pin at \SI{3.3}{V} for \textsf{L}).

\task{D} The RP2040 data sheet specifies $V_{OH}\ge\SI{2.62}{V}$ and $V_{OL}\le\SI{0.5}{V}$ at a load current
equal to the selected drive strength (2, 4, 8 or \SI{12}{mA}) and $IOVDD=\SI{3.3}{V}$. The model delivers
\SI{12.8}{mA} at $V_{OH}=\SI{2.62}{V}$ and sinks \SI{12}{mA} at $V_{OL}=\SI{0.40}{V}$. Which drive strength setting
does the model represent? Drive strength is implemented by switching transistor ``fingers'' in parallel, i.e.\ by
scaling $\beta$ ($I_D\propto\beta$): the \SI{4}{mA} setting needs $\beta_P=\SI{2.5}{mA/V^2}$. What fraction of
the fingers is that, and what does it mean for the switching speed (part 3)?

\begin{solution}
At $V_{OH}=\SI{2.62}{V}$ the model PMOS delivers \SI{12.8}{mA}, the NMOS sinks \SI{12}{mA} at $V_{OL}=\SI{0.4}{V}$:
the model corresponds to the \SI{12}{mA} setting. Since $I_D\propto\beta$ at a fixed operating point,
$\beta_P=\SI{8}{mA/V^2}\cdot 4/12.8=\SI{2.5}{mA/V^2}$ meets the \SI{4}{mA} setting, i.e.\ about one third of
the fingers.
\end{solution}

\task{S/D} A red LED ($V_F\approx\SI{2.0}{V}$) with a \SI{100}{\ohm} series resistor is connected from the pin to
GND. The naive calculation gives $I=(\SI{3.3}{V}-\SI{2.0}{V})/\SI{100}{\ohm}=\SI{13}{mA}$. Draw the load line
$I=(V_{PIN}-V_F)/R$ into your measured characteristic (it passes through \SI{2.0}{V}/\SI{0}{mA} and
\SI{3.3}{V}/\SI{13}{mA}) and read the operating point. The model gives $V_{PIN}=\SI{2.86}{V}$ and $I=\SI{8.6}{mA}$.
Why is the current a third lower than the naive value? Optional: verify it by replacing the sweep source with an
LED and a resistor (\emph{Draw $\rightarrow$ Outputs and Labels $\rightarrow$ Add LED}).

\begin{solution}
Intersection with the PMOS triode curve: $V_{SD}=\SI{0.44}{V}$, i.e.\ $V_{PIN}=\SI{2.86}{V}$ and
$I=\SI{8.6}{mA}$, a third less than the naive value. The output resistance of the pin is not negligible
compared to \SI{100}{\ohm}; for a defined LED current either the resistor must be larger (pin resistance becomes
negligible) or the output stage must be included in the calculation.
\end{solution}

\task{D} Look at the curve below \SI{0}{V} and above \SI{3.3}{V}. Which element conducts there? Relate this to the
absolute maximum rating ``pin voltage $-0.5\ldots IOVDD+\SI{0.5}{V}$'' of the RP2040 and to the term
\emph{injection current}. Why can a large injection current upset an analogue measurement or even a digital
circuit on the same chip?

\begin{solution}
Above $V_{DD}$ the body diode of the PMOS (and in a real chip also the ESD clamp diode) conducts into the supply;
below \SI{0}{V} the body diode of the NMOS conducts from GND. From about \SI{0.4}{V} beyond the rails the current
rises exponentially (in the model to about \SI{9}{mA} at \SI{3.7}{V}). The limits $\pm\SI{0.5}{V}$ keep these
diodes nearly off. Injected current flows as minority carriers into the substrate or into the supply rail: it can
shift the ADC reference and neighbouring channels, trigger the parasitic thyristor of the CMOS structure
(latch-up), or raise $V_{DD}$ (see part~\ref{sec:overvoltage}).
\end{solution}

\task{S/D} Read the maximum power in the transistors from the right scope (model: \SI{96}{mW} in the PMOS for a
short circuit to GND, \SI{110}{mW} at \SI{-0.5}{V}). Many microcontrollers allow \SIrange{50}{100}{mA} in total for
all pins of one supply domain. What does this mean for the number of pins that may drive LEDs at the same time,
and what happens to the chip temperature if a pin is shorted to GND?

\begin{solution}
In a short circuit to GND the PMOS dissipates $\SI{3.3}{V}\cdot\SI{29}{mA}\approx\SI{96}{mW}$ (up to
\SI{110}{mW} at $-0.5$\,V). One pin is survivable, but the total current budget (e.g.\ \SI{50}{mA}) allows only
about five LEDs at \SI{8.6}{mA}. The power is dissipated in a tiny area at the pad; together with metal
migration in the supply lines this is why per-pin and per-domain current limits exist. Larger loads need an
external driver (part~\ref{sec:oc}).
\end{solution}

\task{P/S} Output A drives high, output B drives low. The line voltage follows from $I_{D,P,A}(V)=I_{D,N,B}(V)$;
both transistors work in the triode region. Measure the values of Table~2.1 in the simulation.

\begin{results}{Table 2.1: contention between two push-pull outputs}{lcccc}
  & line voltage & current & power PMOS A & power NMOS B\\ \midrule
  model & \SI{1.09}{V} & \SI{28.2}{mA} & \SI{62}{mW} & \SI{31}{mW}\\
  simulation & \simv{1.09} & \simv{28.2} & \simv{62.3} & \simv{30.8}\\
\end{results}

\begin{solution}
Both transistors are in the triode region. $\beta_P[2.7\,(3.3-V)-\tfrac12(3.3-V)^2]=\beta_N[2.7\,V-\tfrac12V^2]$
gives $V=\SI{1.09}{V}$ and $I=\SI{28.2}{mA}$. Power: $P_{P,A}=(3.3-1.09)\,\si{V}\cdot\SI{28.2}{mA}=\SI{62}{mW}$,
$P_{N,B}=\SI{31}{mW}$.
\end{solution}

\task{D} What logic level does a receiver see on the line?

\begin{solution}
Simulation: \SI{28.20}{mA}, \SI{1.09}{V}, \SI{62.3}{mW} and \SI{30.8}{mW}. \SI{1.09}{V} lies between
$V_{IL,max}$ and $V_{IH,min}$ of a \SI{3.3}{V} CMOS input (about 0.8--2.0\,V): the level is undefined.
\end{solution}

\task{D} Name three situations in embedded practice where this happens. How do tri-state outputs, open-drain
outputs and series resistors prevent the damage? (A series resistor of about \SI{290}{\ohm} in each output
limits the contention current to \SI{5}{mA}: $2R+R_{on,P}+R_{on,N}=\SI{3.3}{V}/\SI{5}{mA}$. What does it cost?)

\begin{solution}
Examples: two masters on a shared line (bus arbitration error), a bidirectional data line where both sides
drive at the same time during the turn-around, bit-banged I$^2$C with the pin configured push-pull, a debugger
and the board both driving reset, two boards connected with swapped TX lines, a pin shorted to its neighbour.
Tri-state: only one driver is enabled at a time (protocol must guarantee it). Open drain: nobody drives high, so a
conflict only produces a low level. Series resistors: $2R+R_{on,P}+R_{on,N}\ge \SI{3.3}{V}/\SI{5}{mA}=\SI{660}{\ohm}$
$\Rightarrow R\approx\SI{290}{\ohm}$ per output (\SI{330}{\ohm} standard value), at the cost of speed (part 3).
\end{solution}

\section{Push-pull output: switching speed and dynamic power}
%======================================================================
\circuitlink{\linkB}{B\_pushpull\_capacitive\_load.txt}

The square wave is the program toggling the pin. The predriver is a logic inverter with an adjustable slew rate
(how fast the gate voltage changes). The output drives the load capacitance $C_L$ (pin, trace, inputs of other
chips). An ammeter measures the current taken from the supply.

\schematic[0.55]{B}

\task{P} Table~3.1 gives the rise and fall times (\SIrange{10}{90}{\percent}) from two models: (a) the RC
approximation $t_r=2.2\,R_{on}C_L$, (b) the square-law model, in which the transistor starts in saturation
(constant current $I_{sat}$) and enters the triode region when $V_{DS}=V_{GS}-V_T$ (integration of
$dt=C_L\,dV/I_D(V)$). Why is the RC approximation too optimistic?

\begin{solution}
(a) $t_r=2.2\cdot\SI{46.3}{\ohm}\cdot C_L$, $t_f=2.2\cdot\SI{30.9}{\ohm}\cdot C_L$: \SI{1.0}{ns}/\SI{0.68}{ns},
\SI{10.2}{ns}/\SI{6.8}{ns}, \SI{102}{ns}/\SI{68}{ns}.\\
(b) Rising edge, $a=V_{GS}-V_T=\SI{2.7}{V}$: from \SI{0.33}{V} to \SI{0.6}{V} the PMOS is saturated,
$t_1=C_L\cdot\SI{0.27}{V}/\SI{29.2}{mA}$; then triode from $V_{SD}=\SI{2.7}{V}$ down to \SI{0.33}{V}:
\[
  t_2=\frac{C_L}{\beta}\int_{0.33}^{2.7}\frac{dx}{x(a-x/2)}=\frac{C_L}{\beta a}\left[\ln\frac{x}{2a-x}\right]_{0.33}^{2.7}
     =\frac{C_L}{\beta}\cdot\SI{1.012}{V^{-1}}.
\]
Result $t_r=\SI{1.36}{ns}$, \SI{13.6}{ns}, \SI{136}{ns} and $t_f=\SI{0.90}{ns}$, \SI{9.0}{ns}, \SI{90}{ns}.
The RC estimate is about \SI{25}{\percent} too optimistic, because at the start of the edge the transistor
delivers only $I_{sat}$ instead of $V_{DD}/R_{on}$.
\end{solution}

\task{S} Set the predriver slew rate to its maximum and measure $t_r$ and $t_f$ for the three capacitances (set the
scope time base accordingly, or zoom in with Run/STOP). For which $C_L$ does the predriver dominate the edge?

\begin{results}{Table 3.1: rise and fall times at the pin}{lcccccc}
  & \multicolumn{2}{c}{$C_L=\SI{10}{pF}$} & \multicolumn{2}{c}{$C_L=\SI{100}{pF}$} & \multicolumn{2}{c}{$C_L=\SI{1}{nF}$}\\
  & $t_r$ & $t_f$ & $t_r$ & $t_f$ & $t_r$ & $t_f$\\ \midrule
  (a) RC model & \SI{1.0}{ns} & \SI{0.68}{ns} & \SI{10.2}{ns} & \SI{6.8}{ns} & \SI{102}{ns} & \SI{68}{ns}\\
  (b) square-law model & \SI{1.36}{ns} & \SI{0.90}{ns} & \SI{13.6}{ns} & \SI{9.0}{ns} & \SI{136}{ns} & \SI{90}{ns}\\
  simulation & \simv{1.5} & \simv{1.1} & \simv{13.6} & \simv{9.1} & \simv{136} & \simv{90}\\
\end{results}

\begin{solution}
Simulation (slew \SI{2}{V/ns} or more): \SI{1.5}{ns}/\SI{1.1}{ns}, \SI{13.6}{ns}/\SI{9.1}{ns},
\SI{136}{ns}/\SI{90}{ns}, i.e.\ exactly model (b) for \SI{100}{pF} and \SI{1}{nF}. At \SI{10}{pF} and below
the gate itself needs about \SI{1.5}{ns} to swing, so the predriver dominates.
\end{solution}

\task{S/D} Increase the toggle frequency with $C_L=\SI{1}{nF}$ until the pin no longer reaches valid logic levels
($V_{IH}=0.7\,V_{DD}$, $V_{IL}=0.3\,V_{DD}$). Compare with the rule of thumb $f_{max}\approx 1/(2\,t_r)\approx
\SI{3.7}{MHz}$ (slower edge). Why do data sheets specify the maximum GPIO frequency only together with a load
capacitance?

\begin{solution}
Each half period must be long enough for the output to cross the threshold: roughly $f_{max}\approx
1/(2\,t_r)$ with the slower edge, here $\approx\SI{3.7}{MHz}$ for \SI{1}{nF}; beyond that the swing shrinks and
at about \SIrange{5}{6}{MHz} the levels are no longer valid. Since $t_r\propto C_L$, a frequency without a
load capacitance is meaningless (typical: ``\SI{50}{MHz} at \SI{30}{pF}'').
\end{solution}

\task{S} Set $f=\SI{1}{MHz}$ and the fastest slew rate. Read the average supply current (right scope) for the three
capacitances and compare with $I=C_LV_{DD}f$. The energy taken from the supply per cycle is $C_LV_{DD}^2$,
independent of $R_{on}$. Where is it dissipated?

\begin{results}{Table 3.2: average supply current at \SI{1}{MHz}}{lccc}
  & $C_L=\SI{10}{pF}$ & $C_L=\SI{100}{pF}$ & $C_L=\SI{1}{nF}$\\ \midrule
  $C_LV_{DD}f$ & \SI{33}{\micro A} & \SI{330}{\micro A} & \SI{3.3}{mA}\\
  simulation & \simv{34} & \simv{330} & \simv{3300}\\
\end{results}

\begin{solution}
Simulation: \SI{34}{\micro A}, \SI{330}{\micro A}, \SI{3300}{\micro A}; formula: \SI{33}{\micro A},
\SI{330}{\micro A}, \SI{3300}{\micro A}. During charging the supply delivers $Q V_{DD}=C_LV_{DD}^2$; half of it is
stored in $C_L$, the other half is converted to heat in the PMOS, whatever its resistance. During discharge the
stored half is dissipated in the NMOS. $P=C_LV_{DD}^2f$ is the dynamic power of all CMOS logic.
\end{solution}

\task{S/D} Set $C_L=\SI{1}{pF}$ and reduce the predriver slew rate to \SI{0.02}{V/ns}. Observe the supply current.
Both transistors conduct while $V_T<V_G<V_{DD}-V_T$ (\SIrange{0.6}{2.7}{V}), at \SI{0.02}{V/ns} that is
\SI{105}{ns} per edge. Measure the average supply current and compare it with $C_LV_{DD}f=\SI{3.3}{\micro A}$
(simulation: \underline{\hspace{2cm}}\simv{376\,µA}). Why do microcontroller input pins have Schmitt triggers, and
why does a slowly changing analogue voltage on a digital input increase the current consumption?

\begin{solution}
Both transistors conduct while $V_T<V_G<V_{DD}-V_T$, i.e.\ \SIrange{0.6}{2.7}{V}; at \SI{0.02}{V/ns} this takes
\SI{105}{ns} per edge. A cross-conduction (shoot-through) current flows directly from $V_{DD}$ to GND (peak
\SI{5.8}{mA}). The average supply current rises from \SI{6}{\micro A} to \SI{376}{\micro A}, more than
100 times $C_LV_{DD}f=\SI{3.3}{\micro A}$. An input stage is also a CMOS inverter: a slow input edge or an analogue
voltage near $V_{DD}/2$ keeps both transistors on (static cross current) and may make the output oscillate.
A Schmitt trigger switches quickly and only once; unused or analogue pins should have the digital input buffer
disabled.
\end{solution}

by I$^2$C Hs-mode masters. All of them must not fight against a device that pulls low.
\end{solution}

directions for I$^2$C; dedicated translators (e.g.\ PCA9306, TXS0102).
\end{solution}

clamping, reflection at the driver.

\begin{results}{Table 7.1: reflections for $R_S=0$ ($R_{on,P}=1/(\SI{25}{mA/V^2}\cdot\SI{2.7}{V})=\SI{15}{\ohm}$)}{lc}
  edge time / round trip & $<\SI{1}{ns}$ / \SI{2}{ns}: electrically long\\
  $\Gamma_S=(R_{on}+R_S-Z_0)/(R_{on}+R_S+Z_0)$ & $(15-50)/(15+50)=-0.54$\\
  $\Gamma_L$ (open end, \SI{5}{pF} only rounds the edge) & $\approx+1$\\
  launched step $V_{DD}\,Z_0/(Z_0+R_{on})$ & \SI{2.54}{V}\\
  first voltage at RX (before clamping) & $2\cdot\SI{2.54}{V}=\SI{5.1}{V}$\\
  matched series resistor $R_S=Z_0-R_{on}$ & $\approx\SI{36}{\ohm}$ (\SI{33}{\ohm} standard value)\\
\end{results}

\begin{solution}
In its fastest setting the output edge takes well below \SI{1}{ns}, the round trip takes
\SI{2}{ns}: the trace is electrically long. With $R_{on,P}=1/(\SI{25}{mA/V^2}\cdot\SI{2.7}{V})=\SI{15}{\ohm}$:
$\Gamma_S=(15-50)/(15+50)=-0.54$, $\Gamma_L\approx+1$ (open end; the \SI{5}{pF} only rounds the edge).
Launched step $\SI{3.3}{V}\cdot 50/(50+15)=\SI{2.54}{V}$; at the receiver it doubles to \SI{5.1}{V}, i.e.\
\SI{1.8}{V} above $V_{DD}$, so the ESD diode clamps it. The reflected wave returns to the driver, is reflected
with $-0.54$ and arrives as an undershoot of about $-0.54\cdot\SI{2.5}{V}$ after another \SI{2}{ns}
(again limited by the GND clamp): the edge rings with a period of \SI{4}{ns}.
\end{solution}

\task{S/D} Set $R_S$ back to 0 and reduce the slew rate. From which slew rate on does the ringing disappear?
Why should you select the slowest slew rate that meets the timing of your interface?

\begin{solution}
The slider sets the slew rate of the gate; the output switches during only part of the gate swing, so
measure the edge at PIN. At \SI{1}{V/ns} the ringing is unchanged (ESD currents \SI{19}{mA}/\SI{24}{mA}),
at \SI{0.3}{V/ns} it is reduced (\SI{4.5}{mA}/\SI{6.6}{mA}), at \SI{0.1}{V/ns} (output edge several
\si{ns}, much longer than the \SI{2}{ns} round trip) the trace behaves like a lumped capacitance and the edge
is clean. A slow edge also has a narrower spectrum: less crosstalk, less radiated EMI, smaller ground bounce
when many pins switch together, and less supply current (part 3). The fastest setting is only needed when the
timing budget of the interface requires it.
\end{solution}

\task{P/S/D} A \SI{3.3}{V} microcontroller board has to
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item read a \SI{5}{V} sensor over I$^2$C at \SI{400}{kHz}; the cable is \SI{30}{cm} long, the total bus
        capacitance is estimated at \SI{250}{pF},
  \item switch a \SI{24}{V}/\SI{50}{mA} solenoid valve at up to \SI{10}{Hz},
  \item send a \SI{10}{MHz} SPI clock over a \SI{10}{cm} trace to a flash memory.
\end{enumerate}
For each signal choose the output type and configuration of the pin (push-pull/open drain, drive strength, slew
rate), dimension the external components and verify your choice in a simulation that you build from the given
circuits. Document the remaining risks.

\begin{solution}
A possible solution (other justified solutions are equally valid):
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item I$^2$C: open drain is mandatory (wired AND, clock stretching, ACK). The RP2040 pins are not
        \SI{5}{V} tolerant $\Rightarrow$ a BSS138 level shifter per line (or a PCA9306) with pull-ups on both
        sides. The cable (\SI{250}{pF}) is on the \SI{5}{V} side: $R_{P,max}=\SI{300}{ns}/(0.8473\cdot\SI{250}{pF})
        =\SI{1.42}{k\ohm}$, but $R_{P,min}=(\SI{5}{V}-\SI{0.4}{V})/\SI{3}{mA}=\SI{1.53}{k\ohm}$: no resistor meets
        both. Options: devices that are rated for \SI{6}{mA} at \SI{0.6}{V} ($R_{P,min}=\SI{733}{\ohm}$, choose
        \SI{1.2}{k\ohm}), a rise-time accelerator or bus buffer, or Standard-mode at \SI{100}{kHz}
        ($R_P=\SI{1.8}{k\ohm}\ldots\SI{4.7}{k\ohm}$). On the short \SI{3.3}{V} side \SI{4.7}{k\ohm} is enough.
        Simulation: part D with \SI{5}{V} pull-up and \SI{250}{pF}. Risk: the capacitance estimate; measure
        $t_r$ on the real board.
  \item Solenoid: low-side N-MOSFET (logic level, $V_{DS}\ge\SI{40}{V}$) or ULN2003 channel; GPIO push-pull,
        \SI{100}{\ohm} gate resistor, \SI{100}{k\ohm} pull-down so the valve stays off during reset (pins are
        inputs then). Free-wheeling: diode + Zener (e.g.\ \SI{24}{V}) if the valve must close quickly,
        $V_{DS}\ge 24+24+1\approx\SI{50}{V}$, otherwise a diode alone. Simulation: part F with \SI{24}{V},
        \SI{480}{\ohm} and the valve inductance.
  \item SPI clock: push-pull, moderate drive strength and slew rate, series resistor $R_S=Z_0-R_{on}$
        (e.g.\ \SI{33}{\ohm}) at the MCU. A \SI{10}{cm} trace has \SI{0.67}{ns} delay; with the fastest edge
        it is long, so terminate or slow the edge (still $t_r\ll\SI{50}{ns}$ half period). Simulation: part G with
        the trace delay changed to \SI{0.67}{ns}. Risk: ringing causes double clocking of the flash.
\end{enumerate}
\end{solution}
