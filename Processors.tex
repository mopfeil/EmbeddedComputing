\section{Processors for Embedded Systems}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\nsl{Embedded systems use a wide range of processors, from 8 bit
microcontrollers that cost a few cents to multi-core application processors
that run Linux. The choice depends on the computing power needed, on the
memory, on the peripherals, on the energy budget and, in large series, on
the price. This chapter first describes the classes of processors, typical
applications and the figures used to compare them. It then explains the
architecture concepts that decide how fast and how predictably code runs:
pipelines, caches and the instruction set. After that it looks at the
important architectures (AVR, ARM Cortex-M/R/A, RISC-V, Xtensa and others)
with assembly examples, at the inside of a microcontroller, at the
peripherals and accelerators around the core (interfaces, signal and image
processing, AI, security) and at the semiconductor manufacturers.
Application processors, a
short comparison of microcontrollers and FPGAs and a decision guide complete
the chapter.

\section{Architecture Fundamentals}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\nsl{An \emph{instruction set architecture} (ISA) is the contract between
software and hardware: registers, instructions, data types, addressing modes
and the exception model. A \emph{microarchitecture} is one implementation of
it. The Cortex-M3 and the Cortex-M4 implement almost the same ISA, but very
different processors such as the Hazard3 in the RP2350 and the cores in the
ESP32-C3 implement RISC-V. The ISA decides about $N_{\text{instr}}$ and code
size, the microarchitecture about CPI, clock rate and predictability. The
most important microarchitecture concepts are the pipeline and the memory
hierarchy with its caches.}

\item[{\rot\bf RISC and CISC: the same operation}]~\\[-\lstskip]
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.46\linewidth}
\begin{lstlisting}[language={}]
; CISC (x86): counter++
add dword [counter], 1
; memory operand: read, add,
; write in ONE instruction
\end{lstlisting}
\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.5\linewidth}
\begin{lstlisting}[language={}]
@ RISC (Cortex-M): counter++
ldr  r1, =counter   @ address
ldr  r0, [r1]       @ load
adds r0, r0, #1     @ add in register
str  r0, [r1]       @ store
\end{lstlisting}
\end{minipage}
\bi
	\ite \textbf{RISC} = load/store architecture: arithmetic only on registers, simple instructions of (mostly) fixed length $\Rightarrow$ easy to decode and to pipeline
	\ite many registers: AVR 32 $\times$ 8 bit, Cortex-M 13 $\times$ 32 bit + SP, LR, PC, RISC-V 31 $\times$ 32 bit + \texttt{zero}
	\ite almost all embedded architectures are RISC; Renesas RX is a CISC exception
	\ite for embedded systems, \textbf{code density} matters more than the label: Thumb-2 and RISC-V ``C'' mix 16 and 32 bit instructions
\ei

\section{Architectures and their Particularities}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\nsl{Most new designs today use one of two 32 bit architectures: ARM
Cortex-M, licensed by Arm to almost every semiconductor company, or RISC-V,
an open instruction set that anybody may implement without license fees.
8 bit microcontrollers are still produced in huge numbers for very simple
and cost-sensitive devices, and some application areas have their own
architectures: Xtensa in older Espressif chips, TriCore and RH850 in
automotive control units, C2000 in motor control. The assembly examples
at the end of this section show how the same C code becomes different
machine code.}

\begin{description}
\item[{\rot\bf Overview of embedded architectures}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\begin{center}
{\footnotesize
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{|l|c|L|L|L|}
\hline
\textbf{architecture} & \textbf{bits} & \textbf{owner, license} & \textbf{particularities} & \textbf{typical use} \\
\hline
AVR, PIC, 8051 & 8 & Microchip and many others & simple, robust, 5\,V, tiny RAM & toys, household devices, sensors \\
MSP430 & 16 & TI & extremely low power & metering, battery sensors \\
ARM Cortex-M & 32 & Arm, licensed core & one ecosystem for thousands of chips & almost all MCU applications \\
ARM Cortex-R & 32 (64) & Arm, licensed core & TCM, lock-step, ECC, fast interrupts & storage controllers, automotive, 5G modems \\
ARM Cortex-A & 64 & Arm, licensed core & MMU, caches, out-of-order (larger cores) & Linux devices, smartphones, infotainment \\
RISC-V & 32/64 & open ISA (RISC-V International) & modular, custom extensions, no license fees & MCUs, coprocessors, AI chips, growing fast \\
Xtensa & 32 & Cadence, configurable & register windows, custom instructions & ESP32/ESP32-S3, audio DSPs \\
TriCore, RH850 & 32 & Infineon, Renesas & safety features, automotive certification & engine and brake control units \\
C2000, SHARC & 32 & TI, ADI & DSP features, fast control loops & motor control, power conversion, audio \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}}
\end{center}
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf 8 bit AVR (ATmega328P)}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\bi
	\ite 32 registers of 8 bit, two-stage pipeline, most instructions in one cycle, 16\,MHz on the Arduino Uno
	\ite pure Harvard: 32\,KB flash, 2\,KB SRAM, 1\,KB EEPROM; 5\,V, robust pins, power-down well below 1\,$\mu$A
	\ite interrupts: hardware saves only the PC, the compiler saves the registers $\Rightarrow$ keep handlers short
	\ite limits: 16/32 bit arithmetic needs several instructions, no division, little RAM
	\ite \textbf{use case}: a battery-powered room sensor or a toy -- simple control logic, 5\,V parts, very cheap; and the chip that made embedded programming popular through Arduino
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf Cortex-M instruction set particularities}]~\\[-\lstskip]
\begin{lstlisting}[language={}]
@ if (x >= 0) y = 1; else y = -1;   -- without a branch
      cmp    r0, #0
      ite    ge             @ if-then-else block: next 2 instructions conditional
      movge  r1, #1
      movlt  r1, #-1
\end{lstlisting}
\bi
	\ite \textbf{Thumb-2}: 16 and 32 bit instructions mixed -- good code density
	\ite \texttt{push \{r4-r7, lr\}}, \texttt{ldm}/\texttt{stm}, \texttt{cbz} (compare and branch if zero)
	\ite \textbf{DSP extension} (M4, M7, M33): \texttt{smlabb} (MAC), \texttt{smlad} (2 MACs), saturation \texttt{qadd}, \texttt{ssat}
	\ite \textbf{ARMv8.1-M}: low-overhead loops \texttt{dls}/\texttt{le}, Helium on 128 bit vectors, pointer authentication (PACBTI)
	\ite exception entry in hardware: \texttt{r0}--\texttt{r3}, \texttt{r12}, \texttt{lr}, \texttt{pc}, \texttt{xpsr} are stacked $\Rightarrow$ handlers are normal C functions
	\ite AAPCS: arguments in \texttt{r0}--\texttt{r3}, result in \texttt{r0}, \texttt{r4}--\texttt{r11} preserved
\ei

\end{center}

\item[{\rot\bf Xtensa and other architectures}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\bi
	\ite \textbf{Xtensa} (Cadence): configurable cores with own instructions -- ESP32 (LX6), ESP32-S3 (LX7 with vector instructions), audio DSPs (HiFi)
	\bi
		\ite \textbf{register windows}: \texttt{entry}/\texttt{retw} rotate a window of 16 out of 64 registers -- fewer pushes and pops
		\ite zero-overhead \texttt{loop}, MAC16 unit
	\ei
	\ite \textbf{Infineon TriCore} (AURIX): MCU + DSP in one, up to 6 cores, lock-step, HSM -- engine and chassis ECUs
	\ite \textbf{Renesas RH850} (automotive), \textbf{Renesas RX} (CISC, 1--8 byte instructions)

	\ite \textbf{Synopsys ARC}: configurable, hidden inside SoCs (sensor hubs, SSD controllers)

\item[{\rot\bf Assembly: the same C function on four processors}]~\\[-\lstskip]
\begin{lstlisting}[language=C]
uint32_t sum(const uint16_t *a, int n) {
    uint32_t s = 0;
    for (int i = 0; i < n; i++) {
        s += a[i];
    }
    return s;
}
\end{lstlisting}
\begin{center}
\resizebox{0.85\textwidth}{!}{%
\begin{tabular}{|l|c|c|l|}
\hline
\textbf{processor} & \textbf{instructions in the loop} & \textbf{code size} & \textbf{reason} \\
\hline
AVR (8 bit) & 12 & 40 bytes & 32 bit sum and 16 bit index need several 8 bit operations \\
Cortex-M0+ & 7 & 24 bytes & 16 bit Thumb instructions \\
Cortex-M4 & 6 & 24 bytes & load with scaled index: \texttt{ldrh r4, [r0, r3, lsl \#1]} \\
RISC-V RV32IMC & 7 & 28 bytes & mix of 16 and 32 bit instructions \\
\hline
\end{tabular}}
\end{center}
\bi
	\ite compiled with \texttt{-Os} (optimise for size); listings and script in \texttt{examples/\-processors},\\ or try it on \url{https://godbolt.org}
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf The loop in assembly: AVR and Cortex-M0+}]~\\[-\lstskip]
\begin{lstlisting}[language={}]
; AVR: s in r22..r25 (4 x 8 bit), pointer in Z (r31:r30), i in r19:r18
.L3:  ld   r26,Z+            ; low byte of a[i], Z++
      ld   r27,Z+            ; high byte of a[i], Z++
      add  r22,r26           ; s += a[i], byte by byte with carry
      adc  r23,r27
      adc  r24,__zero_reg__
      adc  r25,__zero_reg__
      subi r18,-1            ; i++ (16 bit)
      sbci r19,-1
.L2:  cp   r18,r20           ; i < n ?
      cpc  r19,r21
      brlt .L3
\end{lstlisting}
\begin{lstlisting}[language={}]
@ Cortex-M0+: a in r0, n in r1, i in r3, s in r2
.L3:  lsls  r4, r3, #1       @ byte offset = i * 2
      ldrh  r4, [r0, r4]     @ load halfword a[i]
      adds  r3, r3, #1       @ i++
      adds  r2, r2, r4       @ s += a[i]
      b     .L2
.L2:  cmp   r3, r1           @ i < n ?
      blt   .L3
\end{lstlisting}
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf The loop in assembly: RISC-V}]~\\[-\lstskip]
\begin{lstlisting}[language={}]
# RISC-V: a in a4 (copied from a0), n in a1, i in a5, s in a0 (= return value)
.L2:  blt   a5,a1,.L3        # i < n ? compare and branch in one instruction
      ret
.L3:  slli  a3,a5,1          # byte offset = i * 2
      add   a3,a4,a3         # address = a + offset
      lhu   a3,0(a3)         # load halfword unsigned
      addi  a5,a5,1          # i++
      add   a0,a0,a3         # s += a[i]
      j     .L2
\end{lstlisting}
\bi
	\ite RISC-V has no status flags: compare and branch are one instruction (\texttt{blt})
	\ite only one addressing mode (register + 12 bit offset) -- the address is computed with normal instructions
	\ite calling convention: \texttt{a0}--\texttt{a7} arguments and results, \texttt{s0}--\texttt{s11} preserved, \texttt{sp}, \texttt{ra} return address, \texttt{zero}
	\ite more about assembly programming in chapter Programming
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf A DSP kernel: dot product in fixed point}]~\\[-\lstskip]
\begin{lstlisting}[language=C]
int32_t dot_q15(const int16_t *a, const int16_t *b, int n) {
    int32_t s = 0;
    for (int i = 0; i < n; i++)
        s += (int32_t)a[i] * b[i];
    return s;
}
\end{lstlisting}
\begin{center}
\resizebox{0.95\textwidth}{!}{%
\begin{tabular}{|l|c|l|}
\hline
\textbf{processor} & \textbf{instr.\ in loop} & \textbf{what the compiler uses (\texttt{-O2})} \\
\hline
Cortex-M0+ & 7 & \texttt{ldrsh}, \texttt{muls}, \texttt{adds}, compare and branch \\
RISC-V RV32IMC & 7 & \texttt{lh}, \texttt{mul}, \texttt{add} -- no multiply-accumulate in the base ISA \\
Cortex-M4 / M33 & 5 & \texttt{ldrh} with pointer increment, \texttt{smlabb}: 16 $\times$ 16 + 32 bit in one instruction \\
Cortex-M55 & 4 & as M4, plus low-overhead loop \texttt{dls}/\texttt{le}: no compare, no branch \\
Xtensa LX7 (ESP32-S3) & 5 & zero-overhead \texttt{loop}, MAC16 \texttt{mula.aa.ll} into the accumulator \texttt{ACCLO} \\
\hline
\end{tabular}}
\end{center}
\bi
	\ite \textbf{use case}: audio filter, motor current control, vibration analysis -- the inner loop is a MAC
	\ite SIMD (\texttt{smlad}: two MACs at once) usually needs intrinsics or CMSIS-DSP
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf The DSP loop in assembly: Cortex-M4 and Xtensa}]~\\[-\lstskip]
\begin{lstlisting}[language={}]
@ Cortex-M4: r3 -> a, r1 -> b, r2 = end of a, s in r0
.L3:  ldrh    lr, [r3, #2]!     @ load a[i], pointer += 2 (pre-increment)
      ldrh    ip, [r1, #2]!     @ load b[i], pointer += 2
      cmp     r3, r2            @ end reached?
      smlabb  r0, lr, ip, r0    @ s += a[i] * b[i] (bottom halves, signed)
      bne     .L3
\end{lstlisting}
\begin{lstlisting}[language={}]
# Xtensa LX7: a8 = iteration count, a9 -> a, a3 -> b
      wsr     a10, ACCLO        # accumulator = 0
      loop    a8, .L3_LEND      # hardware loop: repeat a8 times
.L3:  l16ui   a11, a9, 0        # load a[i]
      l16ui   a10, a3, 0        # load b[i]
      addi.n  a9, a9, 2
      mula.aa.ll a11, a10       # ACC += a[i] * b[i]
      addi.n  a3, a3, 2
.L3_LEND:
      rsr     a2, ACCLO         # result from the accumulator
\end{lstlisting}
\bi
	\ite the compiler schedules \texttt{cmp} before \texttt{smlabb} so that the flags are ready for \texttt{bne} without a stall
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf Floating point: software, FPU and vector unit}]~\\[-\lstskip]
\begin{lstlisting}[language={}]
@ Cortex-M0+ (no FPU): every operation is a library call of 30-100 cycles
      bl    __aeabi_fmul        @ a[i] * b[i]
      bl    __aeabi_fadd        @ s + product
@ Cortex-M4F: fused multiply-add in the FPU, 3 cycles
      vfma.f32  s0, s14, s15    @ s0 += s14 * s15
@ Cortex-M55 with -ffast-math: Helium, 4 floats per instruction
.L14: vldrw.32  q3, [ip], #16   @ load a[i..i+3]
      vldrw.32  q1, [r3], #16   @ load b[i..i+3]
      vmul.f32  q3, q3, q1
      vadd.f32  q2, q2, q3      @ 4 partial sums
      le        lr, .L14        @ low-overhead loop end
\end{lstlisting}
\bi
	\ite without \texttt{-ffast-math} the M55 adds the products one by one: float addition is not associative, and C requires the source order
	\ite ESP32-C3 (RV32IMC) calls \texttt{\_\_mulsf3}/\texttt{\_\_addsf3}; ESP32-S3 has an FPU (\texttt{madd.s}); RP2040 has fast float routines in its boot ROM
\ei
\os{\newpage}
\end{description}

\section{Inside a Microcontroller}

\nsl{The processor core is only a small part of a microcontroller. Around it
are memories of different kinds, a bus system that connects the core with
the peripherals, a clock system and a power management unit. How these parts
work determines how fast a program really runs, how exactly its timing can
be predicted and how long a battery lasts.}

\item[{\rot\bf Memories}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\bi
	\ite \textbf{flash}: program memory; erased in blocks, 10\,000--100\,000 write cycles; too slow for high clocks $\Rightarrow$ wait states, prefetch, cache
	\ite \textbf{XIP}: RP2040 and ESP32 execute directly from external QSPI flash through a cache -- a miss costs many cycles
	\ite \textbf{SRAM}: data, stack, time-critical code
	\ite \textbf{EEPROM} or flash emulation: settings, calibration
	\ite \textbf{MRAM, RRAM, FRAM}: byte-writable non-volatile memories in recent MCUs (22\,nm and below, where embedded flash is hard to build)
	\ite \textbf{MPU}: protects memory regions (stack overflow!); an MMU with virtual memory only on application processors

\section{Manufacturers and their Product Lines}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\nsl{Processor cores and chips usually come from different companies. IP
vendors such as Arm, SiFive, Andes or Cadence design cores and license them.
Semiconductor manufacturers combine licensed or own cores with memories,
peripherals and analog circuits and sell the chips. The market has been
consolidated by acquisitions: Microchip bought Atmel (2016), NXP merged with
Freescale (2015), Infineon bought Cypress (2020), Renesas bought Dialog
(2021). Because the cores are similar, the manufacturers differ mainly in
their peripherals, in their focus markets, in their software tools and in
how long they deliver a chip.}

\section{Application Processors and Heterogeneous SoCs}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\nsl{When an application needs a graphical user interface, a camera, a lot
of memory or complex network software, a microcontroller is no longer enough.
Application processors run an operating system with virtual memory such as
Linux. Most of them are heterogeneous systems on chip: next to the
application cores they contain microcontroller cores for real-time tasks,
graphics and video units and, more and more, accelerators for neural
networks.}

\begin{description}
\item[{\rot\bf Application processors}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\bi
	\ite ARM Cortex-A (and RISC-V application cores) with \textbf{MMU} (virtual memory, required by Linux), several cores at GHz, L1/L2/L3 caches, external DRAM
	\ite examples: Raspberry Pi 5 (4 $\times$ Cortex-A76, 2.4\,GHz), NXP i.MX\,93/95, TI AM62x, STM32MP2, Rockchip RK3588
	\ite RISC-V application cores arriving: SiFive, T-Head, SpacemiT; Linux distributions target RVA23
	\ite GPU, video codecs, image signal processor, NPU on the same chip
	\ite non-deterministic timing (caches, DRAM, OS) $\Rightarrow$ hard real time on a separate core or MCU
	\ite \textbf{use case}: a machine HMI with touch display, web server and OPC UA -- Linux on Cortex-A, the drive control on a separate MCU core
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf Heterogeneous SoC: Linux and real time on one chip}]~\\[-\lblskip]

\section{Excursion: Microcontroller or FPGA?}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\nsl{A processor executes instructions one after the other; even a
superscalar core does only a few things at the same time. A field
programmable gate array (FPGA) contains no fixed processor, but thousands of
small configurable logic blocks and a configurable network between them. The
algorithm is turned into a circuit, and all parts of this circuit work in
parallel in every clock cycle. This section compares the two approaches and
shows where they meet.}

\begin{description}
\item[{\rot\bf What is inside an FPGA}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\begin{figure}[!hbtp]
\begin{center}
\resizebox{0.75\textwidth}{!}{%
\begin{tikzpicture}[font=\sffamily\small,>=Stealth,
	b/.style={draw,thick,minimum height=12mm,align=center}]
	\foreach \i in {0,...,3} \draw[->,thick] (-1.2,1.05-\i*0.3) -- (0,1.05-\i*0.3);
	\node[left,font=\sffamily\scriptsize] at (-1.2,0.6) {inputs};
	\node[b,fill=rwucyan!20,minimum width=22mm,minimum height=16mm] (lut) at (1.1,0.6) {LUT\\(16 $\times$ 1 bit\\memory)};
	\node[b,fill=rwuviolet!20,minimum width=14mm] (ff) at (4,0.9) {D flip-\\flop};
	\node[draw,thick,trapezium,trapezium angle=70,shape border rotate=270,minimum width=6mm,minimum height=14mm,fill=gray!10] (mux) at (6,0.6) {};
	\draw[->,thick] (lut.east) -- ++(0.5,0) |- (ff.west);
	\draw[->,thick] (lut.east) -- ++(0.5,0) |- ([yshift=-3mm]mux.west);
	\draw[->,thick] (ff.east) -- ([yshift=3mm]mux.west);
	\draw[->,thick] (mux.east) -- ++(1,0) node[right,font=\sffamily\scriptsize]{to routing};
	\node[font=\sffamily\scriptsize] at (4,-0.3) {clock};
	\draw[->,thick] (4,-0.15) -- (ff.south);
\end{tikzpicture}}
\caption{Basic logic element: look-up table, flip-flop, multiplexer}
\end{center}
\end{figure}
\bi
	\ite a 4- or 6-input \textbf{look-up table} (LUT) realises any logic function of its inputs; thousands to millions of them
	\ite plus programmable routing, \textbf{block RAM}, \textbf{DSP slices}, PLLs, fast transceivers
	\ite configuration loaded from flash at power-up (SRAM-based) or stored on chip (flash-based, e.g.\ Microchip PolarFire)
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf Sequential versus parallel}]~\\[-\lstskip]
\begin{lstlisting}[language=C]
// microcontroller: the CPU does one thing after the other
while (1) {
    cnt++;
    gpio_put(LED, (cnt >> 23) & 1);
}
\end{lstlisting}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Verilog]
// FPGA: describes a circuit - a 24 bit counter register and a wire
module blink(input clk, output led);
  reg [23:0] cnt = 0;
  always @(posedge clk) cnt <= cnt + 1;
  assign led = cnt[23];
endmodule
\end{lstlisting}
\bi
	\ite 100 such counters on the MCU: 100 times slower; on the FPGA: 100 copies of the circuit, all at full speed
	\ite Verilog/VHDL is synthesised into LUTs and flip-flops -- a hardware description, not a program
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf Microcontroller and FPGA compared}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\begin{center}
{\footnotesize
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{|l|L|L|}
\hline
 & \textbf{microcontroller} & \textbf{FPGA} \\
\hline
execution & sequential instructions & parallel circuits \\
timing & $\mu$s, interrupt latency and jitter & ns, cycle-exact and deterministic \\
throughput & limited by the clock and the core & scales with the chip size (parallelism) \\
languages & C, C++, Rust, MicroPython & VHDL, Verilog, SystemVerilog; HLS from C++; Chisel, Amaranth \\
development time & short; debugging with breakpoints & long; simulation, timing closure, synthesis takes minutes to hours \\
power & $\mu$W in sleep & static power, usually mW to W; no deep sleep (exceptions: iCE40, Lattice CrossLink) \\
price & from 0.10\,\texteuro{} & from a few euros; large devices hundreds to thousands of euros \\
change after delivery & firmware update & reconfiguration (also a firmware update) \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}}
\end{center}
\bi
	\ite \textbf{use cases for FPGAs}: image processing at line rate, high-speed ADC interfaces, many parallel PWM/encoder channels, protocol bridges, EtherCAT/TSN, ASIC prototypes
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf Decision: MCU, PIO or FPGA?}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\begin{center}

	\ite \textbf{example}: 20\,kHz motor PWM $\rightarrow$ MCU; 8 WS2812 LED strips $\rightarrow$ RP2040 PIO; 64-channel ultrasound at 40\,MHz $\rightarrow$ FPGA
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf Where microcontroller and FPGA meet}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\bi
	\ite \textbf{programmable I/O in MCUs}: RP2040/RP2350 PIO state machines (VGA, WS2812, DShot); configurable logic cells (CCL in AVR, CLB in PIC and TI C2000)
	\ite \textbf{soft cores}: AMD MicroBlaze V and Altera Nios V (both RISC-V); open source VexRiscv, NEORV32, PicoRV32
	\ite \textbf{SoC-FPGAs}: AMD Zynq-7000 (Cortex-A9), Zynq UltraScale+ (A53 + R5F), Versal (AI engines); Altera Agilex 5, Cyclone V SoC; Microchip PolarFire SoC (5 RISC-V cores)
	\ite \textbf{small FPGAs with open toolchains}: Lattice iCE40/ECP5, Gowin with Yosys + nextpnr
	\ite rule of thumb: start with an MCU; use an FPGA (or PIO) only for the part the MCU cannot do in time
\ei
\os{\newpage}
\end{description}

\item[{\rot\bf Choosing a processor: checklist}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\bi
	\ite \textbf{function}: peripherals (UARTs, ADC resolution, CAN, USB, Ethernet, radio)
	\ite \textbf{performance}: FPU or DSP? interrupt latency, determinism?
	\ite \textbf{memory}: flash and RAM with reserve (start with at most 50\,\% usage)
	\ite \textbf{energy}: battery or mains? sleep current, wake-up time
	\ite \textbf{environment and safety}: temperature range, qualification, certification
	\ite \textbf{security}: secure boot, TrustZone, crypto, update capability (Cyber Resilience Act)
	\ite \textbf{software}: SDK, RTOS and Rust support, examples, community
	\ite \textbf{business}: price at the planned quantity, 10+ years availability, second source
	\ite in practice the ecosystem and the availability decide more often than the core
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf Exercises}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\bi
	\ite Compile \texttt{sum.c} on \url{https://godbolt.org} for the Cortex-M0+ with \texttt{-O0}, \texttt{-Os} and \texttt{-O2}. Compare the number of instructions in the loop.
	\ite Change the type of \texttt{s} to \texttt{uint16\_t}. Which processor profits most, and why?
	\ite Compile \texttt{dot.c} for the Cortex-M55 with and without \texttt{-ffast-math}. Why does the result of \texttt{dot\_f32} possibly change in the last bits?
	\ite Rewrite \texttt{dot\_q15} with the CMSIS intrinsic \texttt{\_\_SMLAD} so that two products are computed per instruction on the Cortex-M4. What must be true for the arrays?
	\ite A data cache has 32\,KB, 4 ways and 64 byte lines. How many sets does it have, and how many bits are used for tag, index and offset? Continue the cache example table with the addresses 0x44 and 0x00.
	\ite Estimate the battery lifetime of a sensor that wakes up every minute for 50\,ms at 20\,mA and sleeps at 5\,$\mu$A (battery 1000\,mAh).
	\ite A camera delivers 640 $\times$ 480 pixels with 2 bytes at 30\,fps. Which data rate does this need, how much RAM does one frame need, and which of the processors of this lecture could handle it?
	\ite Plan the pins of an RP2040 for one UART, one I$^2$C bus, one SPI bus and four PWM outputs (use the GPIO function table of the datasheet).
	\ite Use the decision tree for: (a) a weather station with Wi-Fi, (b) a 3-phase inverter with 20\,kHz PWM, (c) a camera with person detection, (d) a 64-channel ultrasound front end. Justify with the checklist.
\ei
\end{description}
