\section{Processors for Embedded Systems}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\nsl{Embedded systems use a wide range of processors, from 8 bit
microcontrollers that cost a few cents to multi-core application processors
that run Linux. The choice depends on the computing power needed, on the
memory, on the peripherals, on the energy budget and, in large series, on
the price. This chapter first describes the classes of processors and the
figures used to compare them. It then explains the architecture concepts
that decide how fast and how predictably code runs: pipelines, caches and
the instruction set. After that it looks at the important architectures
(AVR, ARM Cortex-M/R/A, RISC-V, Xtensa and others) with assembly examples,
at the inside of a microcontroller and at the semiconductor manufacturers
and their product lines. Application processors with accelerators and a
short comparison of microcontrollers and FPGAs complete the chapter.
\newpage}

\begin{description}
\item[{\rot\bf Classes of processors}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\bi
	\ite \textbf{microcontroller} (MCU): CPU, flash, RAM and peripherals on one chip
	\bi
		\ite runs without an operating system or with an RTOS; starts in milliseconds; consumes milliwatts or less
		\ite 8 bit (AVR, PIC, 8051) for very simple, cheap devices; 32 bit (ARM Cortex-M, RISC-V) for almost everything else
	\ei
	\ite \textbf{application processor} (MPU): fast cores with memory management unit, external DRAM, usually Linux or Android
	\bi
		\ite ARM Cortex-A (smartphones, Raspberry Pi), increasingly RISC-V application cores
	\ei
	\ite \textbf{real-time processor}: MCU-like determinism at high clock rates with safety features (ARM Cortex-R, Infineon TriCore)
	\ite \textbf{digital signal processor} (DSP): optimised for filter and transformation algorithms (audio, radio, motor control)
	\ite \textbf{FPGA}: configurable logic, for highly parallel or very fast functions; often combined with processor cores (system on chip)
	\ite modern chips combine several of these: e.g.\ Cortex-A cores for Linux plus a Cortex-M core for real time and an NPU for neural networks on the same die
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf The spectrum of embedded processors}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\begin{center}
{\footnotesize
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{|>{\raggedright}p{17mm}|L|>{\raggedright}p{17mm}|L|>{\raggedright}p{15mm}|>{\raggedright}p{15mm}|>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{19mm}|}
\hline
\textbf{class} & \textbf{examples} & \textbf{clock} & \textbf{memory} & \textbf{power} & \textbf{price (approx.)} & \textbf{software} \\
\hline
8 bit MCU & ATmega328P, PIC16, 8051 & 1--20\,MHz & 1--64\,KB flash, $<$\,8\,KB RAM & $\mu$W--mW & $<$\,1\,\texteuro{} & bare metal \\

high-end MCU & STM32H7, i.MX RT1170, STM32N6 & 0.4--1\,GHz & MBs SRAM, external flash/RAM & 0.1--1\,W & 3--20\,\texteuro{} & RTOS \\

\hline
\end{tabularx}}
\end{center}
\bi
	\ite the borders blur: high-end MCUs reach the performance of older application processors, and many chips are heterogeneous
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf Key figures: performance}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\bi
	\ite execution time of a program:
	\[ T = N_{\text{instr}} \cdot \text{CPI} \cdot \frac{1}{f_{\text{clk}}} \]
	\bi
		\ite $N_{\text{instr}}$: number of executed instructions -- depends on the instruction set and the compiler
		\ite CPI: cycles per instruction (or IPC = 1/CPI) -- depends on the microarchitecture (pipeline, caches, wait states)
		\ite $f_{\text{clk}}$: clock frequency -- depends on the technology and on the pipeline depth
	\ei
	\ite the clock alone says little: a 200\,MHz Cortex-M7 can be faster than a 400\,MHz Cortex-M0+
	\ite benchmarks normalised to the clock:
	\bi
		\ite \textbf{DMIPS/MHz} (Dhrystone): old and easy to fool with compiler tricks
		\ite \textbf{CoreMark/MHz} (EEMBC): list processing, matrix, state machine, CRC -- today's standard figure for MCU cores
		\ite application benchmarks: EEMBC ULPMark (energy), MLPerf Tiny (neural networks), own code on an evaluation board
	\ei
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf Key figures: CoreMark of ARM Cortex-M cores}]~\\[-\lblskip]

\bi
	\ite figures published by Arm, measured under ideal conditions (code and data in zero wait state memory, best compiler)
	\ite in a real chip, flash wait states, caches and the bus system can cost a large part of this
	\ite an 8 bit AVR reaches well below one CoreMark/MHz -- 32 bit arithmetic costs several instructions
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf Key figures: everything else that matters}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\bi
	\ite \textbf{determinism}: interrupt latency (cycles from the event to the first instruction of the handler), jitter, worst-case execution time (WCET)
	\ite \textbf{memory}: flash and RAM size, memory speed (wait states), code density of the instruction set
	\ite \textbf{energy}:
	\bi
		\ite active current per MHz (low-power MCUs today: about 15--100\,$\mu$A/MHz)
		\ite sleep current with RTC running (0.1--5\,$\mu$A), wake-up time ($\mu$s to ms), energy per operation
	\ei
	\ite \textbf{peripherals}: ADC/DAC, timers, communication interfaces, radio, security (crypto, secure boot, TrustZone)
	\ite \textbf{environment}: temperature range (consumer 0--70\,\textdegree C, industrial $-$40--85\,\textdegree C, automotive up to 125/150\,\textdegree C), AEC-Q100 qualification
	\ite \textbf{functional safety}: certified for ISO\,26262 (ASIL), IEC\,61508 (SIL): lock-step cores, ECC on memories, self tests
	\ite \textbf{business}: price, long-term availability (many vendors guarantee 10--15 years), second source, software ecosystem
\ei
\os{\newpage}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Architecture Fundamentals}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\nsl{An \emph{instruction set architecture} (ISA) is the contract between
software and hardware: registers, instructions, data types, addressing modes
and the exception model. A \emph{microarchitecture} is one implementation of
it. The Cortex-M3 and the Cortex-M4 implement almost the same ISA, but very
different processors such as the Hazard3 in the RP2350 and the cores in the
ESP32-C3 implement RISC-V. The ISA decides about $N_{\text{instr}}$ and code
size, the microarchitecture about CPI, clock rate and predictability. The
most important microarchitecture concepts are the pipeline and the memory
hierarchy with its caches.}

\begin{description}
\item[{\rot\bf Instruction set and microarchitecture}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\bi
	\ite \textbf{ISA} (instruction set architecture): what the programmer and the compiler see
	\bi
		\ite registers, instructions and their encoding, data types, addressing modes, exceptions and privilege levels
		\ite examples: ARMv6-M, ARMv7-M, ARMv8-M, RV32IMC, AVR, Xtensa LX
	\ei
	\ite \textbf{microarchitecture}: how the ISA is built in hardware
	\bi
		\ite pipeline depth, number of execution units, caches, branch prediction, bus interfaces
		\ite examples: Cortex-M3 and Cortex-M4 (both ARMv7-M), Hazard3 and ESP32-C3 core (both RV32)
	\ei
	\ite the same binary code runs on all cores of an ISA -- only faster or slower
	\ite ISA properties that matter in embedded systems: code density, interrupt model, deterministic timing, availability of compilers, debuggers and RTOS ports
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf Von Neumann and Harvard architecture}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\begin{figure}[!hbtp]
\begin{center}

\bi
	\ite \textbf{Harvard}: fetching the next instruction and accessing data at the same time -- higher throughput
	\bi
		\ite AVR: pure Harvard; constants in flash need special instructions (\texttt{PROGMEM}, \texttt{lpm})
	\ei
	\ite \textbf{modified Harvard} (Cortex-M, RISC-V MCUs): separate instruction and data buses or caches, but \textbf{one} common address space -- a pointer can point to flash or RAM
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf RISC and CISC}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\bi
	\ite \textbf{CISC} (complex instruction set computer, e.g.\ x86, Renesas RX): many instructions, memory operands in arithmetic instructions, variable length
	\ite \textbf{RISC} (reduced instruction set computer): almost all embedded architectures today
	\bi
		\ite \textbf{load/store architecture}: arithmetic only on registers; memory accessed only with load and store
		\ite many general purpose registers (AVR 32 $\times$ 8 bit, Cortex-M 13 $\times$ 32 bit + SP, LR, PC, RISC-V 31 $\times$ 32 bit + zero register)
		\ite simple, mostly fixed length instructions $\Rightarrow$ easy to decode and to pipeline
		\ite compressed instruction sets for small code: ARM Thumb (16 bit), RISC-V ``C'' extension
	\ei
	\ite the difference has become smaller: x86 processors translate their instructions internally into RISC-like micro-operations, and ``RISC'' ISAs have gained DSP, vector and crypto instructions
	\ite for embedded systems, \textbf{code density} matters more than the label: flash costs chip area and money
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf Data width, alignment and byte order}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\bi
	\ite the \textbf{word width} is the width of registers and ALU: 8, 16, 32 or 64 bit
	\bi
		\ite C: \texttt{int} is 16 bit on AVR and MSP430, 32 bit on ARM and RISC-V $\Rightarrow$ use the types of \texttt{stdint.h}: \texttt{uint16\_t}, \texttt{int32\_t}
		\ite address width decides the address space: 16 bit = 64\,KB, 32 bit = 4\,GB
	\ei
	\ite \textbf{alignment}: a 32 bit value at an address divisible by 4
	\bi
		\ite Cortex-M0+ and many RISC-V cores: unaligned access causes a fault (or a slow trap)
		\ite Cortex-M3/M4/M7: unaligned single loads work, but cost extra cycles
		\ite matters for communication buffers and structures declared as \texttt{packed}
	\ei
	\ite \textbf{byte order} (endianness): almost all MCUs today are little endian, network protocols are big endian (\texttt{htons}, \texttt{ntohl})
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\bi
	\ite the stages work in parallel on consecutive instructions $\Rightarrow$ ideally one instruction per clock (CPI = 1)
	\ite a taken branch is only known in the execute stage: the instructions fetched behind it are thrown away (\textbf{branch penalty})
	\ite deeper pipelines allow higher clock rates, but make branches and dependencies more expensive
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf Pipeline hazards}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\bi
	\ite \textbf{data hazard}: an instruction needs the result of the previous one
	\bi
		\ite \textbf{forwarding} passes the result directly from the ALU output to the next instruction
		\ite a load followed by its use often still costs one stall cycle (load-use hazard)
	\ei
	\ite \textbf{control hazard}: branches
	\bi
		\ite Cortex-M0+: a taken branch costs 2 cycles instead of 1; Cortex-M4: 1 + 1\ldots3 cycles refill
		\ite \textbf{branch prediction} guesses the direction (static: ``backwards = taken'', dynamic: history tables); Cortex-M7 and application cores predict dynamically
		\ite compilers help: loop unrolling, conditional execution (ARM \texttt{IT} blocks), \textbf{zero-overhead loops} in DSPs, Xtensa (\texttt{loop}) and ARMv8.1-M (\texttt{dls}/\texttt{le})
	\ei
	\ite \textbf{structural hazard}: two instructions need the same unit, e.g.\ one memory port for instruction fetch and data $\Rightarrow$ reason for Harvard buses
	\ite for real time: a simple pipeline makes the cycle count of a code section easy to predict
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf Superscalar and out-of-order execution}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\bi
	\ite \textbf{superscalar} (dual issue): two instructions start in the same cycle if they are independent
	\bi
		\ite Cortex-M7, Cortex-M85, Cortex-A53/A55, ESP32-P4 cores; IPC up to about 2
	\ei
	\ite \textbf{out-of-order}: the core reorders instructions to fill waiting slots, with register renaming and speculative execution
	\bi
		\ite larger Cortex-A cores (A72, A76 in the Raspberry Pi 5), x86
		\ite much higher performance per MHz, but much more chip area and energy
	\ei
	\ite microcontrollers stay \textbf{in-order}:
	\bi
		\ite execution time depends far less on history $\Rightarrow$ WCET can be analysed
		\ite small, cheap and energy-efficient
	\ei
	\ite speculative execution opened security holes (Spectre, Meltdown, 2018) -- another reason for simple cores in security-critical devices
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf Memory hierarchy}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\begin{center}
{\footnotesize
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{|l|l|L|}
\hline
\textbf{memory} & \textbf{typical access time} & \textbf{remark} \\
\hline
registers & 0 cycles (operand of the instruction) & 16--32 registers \\
TCM (tightly coupled memory) & 1 cycle, always & Cortex-M7/M55/M85, Cortex-R: deterministic \\
on-chip SRAM & 1--2 cycles & bus matrix: conflicts with DMA possible \\
cache hit & 1 cycle & only if the cache is present and the data is in it \\
internal flash & 0--7 wait states & e.g.\ STM32F407 at 168\,MHz: 5 wait states, hidden by prefetch/ART cache \\
external QSPI flash (XIP), cache miss & 20--100+ cycles & RP2040, ESP32: code lives here \\
external DRAM & 50--150\,ns & application processors \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}}
\end{center}
\bi
	\ite the CPU is faster than the memory: without caches or wait-state hiding a 480\,MHz core would mostly wait
	\ite \textbf{locality}: programs use the same addresses again soon (temporal) and neighbouring addresses (spatial) -- caches exploit both
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf How a cache works}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\begin{figure}[!hbtp]
\begin{center}

\bi
	\ite a \textbf{cache line} (16--64 bytes) is loaded as a whole on a miss $\Rightarrow$ spatial locality
	\ite \textbf{direct mapped} (1 way): simple, but two addresses with the same index throw each other out; \textbf{n-way set-associative}: a line can go into n places
	\ite \textbf{write-through}: every write also goes to memory; \textbf{write-back}: only when the line is replaced (faster, but memory is temporarily out of date)
	\ite Cortex-M7: instruction cache 2-way, data cache 4-way, 0--64\,KB each; RP2040: 16\,KB XIP cache for the external flash
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf Caches in embedded practice}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\bi
	\ite \textbf{DMA coherency}: DMA writes to RAM, the CPU still sees the old data in its cache (and vice versa)
	\bi
		\ite before DMA reads a buffer: clean the cache (\texttt{SCB\_CleanDCache\_by\_Addr}); after DMA wrote it: invalidate (\texttt{SCB\_InvalidateDCache\_by\_Addr})
		\ite or mark the buffer as non-cacheable with the MPU -- a classic source of bugs when porting from a Cortex-M4 to an M7
	\ei
	\ite \textbf{timing}: the same code can take very different times depending on the cache content $\Rightarrow$ WCET analysis must assume misses
	\bi
		\ite put interrupt handlers and control loops into TCM or SRAM (\texttt{IRAM\_ATTR} on the ESP32, \texttt{\_\_not\_in\_flash\_func} on the RP2040)
		\ite some cores allow cache locking: lines that are never replaced
	\ei
	\ite \textbf{multi-core}: application processors keep caches of several cores coherent in hardware; on MCUs with two cores (RP2040, RP2350, i.MX RT1170) shared data needs care
\ei
\os{\newpage}
\end{description}

\section{Architectures and their Particularities}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\nsl{Most new designs today use one of two 32 bit architectures: ARM
Cortex-M, licensed by Arm to almost every semiconductor company, or RISC-V,
an open instruction set that anybody may implement without license fees.
8 bit microcontrollers are still produced in huge numbers for very simple
and cost-sensitive devices, and some application areas have their own
architectures: Xtensa in older Espressif chips, TriCore and RH850 in
automotive control units, C2000 in motor control. The assembly examples
at the end of this section show how the same C code becomes different
machine code.}

\begin{description}
\item[{\rot\bf Overview of embedded architectures}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\begin{center}
{\footnotesize
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{|l|c|L|L|L|}
\hline
\textbf{architecture} & \textbf{bits} & \textbf{owner, license} & \textbf{particularities} & \textbf{typical use} \\
\hline
AVR, PIC, 8051 & 8 & Microchip and many others & simple, robust, 5\,V, tiny RAM & toys, household devices, sensors \\
MSP430 & 16 & TI & extremely low power & metering, battery sensors \\
ARM Cortex-M & 32 & Arm, licensed core & one ecosystem for thousands of chips & almost all MCU applications \\
ARM Cortex-R & 32 (64) & Arm, licensed core & TCM, lock-step, ECC, fast interrupts & storage controllers, automotive, 5G modems \\
ARM Cortex-A & 64 & Arm, licensed core & MMU, caches, out-of-order (larger cores) & Linux devices, smartphones, infotainment \\
RISC-V & 32/64 & open ISA (RISC-V International) & modular, custom extensions, no license fees & MCUs, coprocessors, AI chips, growing fast \\
Xtensa & 32 & Cadence, configurable & register windows, custom instructions & ESP32/ESP32-S3, audio DSPs \\
TriCore, RH850 & 32 & Infineon, Renesas & safety features, automotive certification & engine and brake control units \\
C2000, SHARC & 32 & TI, ADI & DSP features, fast control loops & motor control, power conversion, audio \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}}
\end{center}
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf 8 bit AVR (ATmega328P)}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\bi
	\ite 32 registers of 8 bit, most instructions in one clock cycle, 16\,MHz on the Arduino Uno
	\ite two-stage pipeline: the next instruction is fetched while the current one executes
	\ite pure Harvard architecture: 32\,KB flash for code, 2\,KB SRAM for data, 1\,KB EEPROM
	\ite 5\,V operation, robust I/O pins, very low sleep current (well below 1\,$\mu$A in power-down)
	\ite interrupts: the hardware only saves the PC, the compiler saves the registers $\Rightarrow$ short handlers matter
	\ite limits: 16 or 32 bit arithmetic needs several instructions, no hardware division, little RAM
	\ite still used for simple control tasks; and it made embedded programming popular through Arduino
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf ARM: three profiles}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\bi
	\ite \textbf{Cortex-A} (application): AArch64 (64 bit), MMU, multi-level caches, NEON/SVE vector units; Linux, Android
	\bi
		\ite big.LITTLE: fast cores (A76, A78) together with efficient cores (A53, A55) on one chip
	\ei
	\ite \textbf{Cortex-R} (real time): high clock rates \textbf{and} deterministic response
	\bi
		\ite TCM instead of (only) caches, ECC, \textbf{dual-core lock-step} (two cores compute the same, a comparator detects faults)
		\ite Cortex-R5/R52: automotive (ASIL\,D), hard disk/SSD controllers, 4G/5G modems; R52 supports a hypervisor for mixed criticality
	\ei
	\ite \textbf{Cortex-M} (microcontroller): small, energy-efficient, easy to program completely in C or Rust
	\bi
		\ite vector table with addresses, hardware register saving, NVIC, SysTick, SWD debug -- the same on every Cortex-M chip
	\ei
	\ite Arm sells licenses: a core license (e.g.\ Cortex-M33) or an architecture license (own core, e.g.\ Apple, Qualcomm)
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf ARM Cortex-M family}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\begin{center}
\resizebox{\textwidth}{!}{%
\begin{tabular}{|l|l|l|}
\hline
\textbf{core} & \textbf{architecture} & \textbf{features} \\
\hline
Cortex-M0 / M0+ & ARMv6-M & smallest and most efficient; Thumb subset, no division instruction \\
Cortex-M3 & ARMv7-M & full Thumb-2 instruction set, hardware division \\
Cortex-M4 & ARMv7E-M & + DSP instructions (SIMD, MAC), optional single precision FPU \\
Cortex-M7 & ARMv7E-M & superscalar, caches, TCM, optional double precision FPU, up to about 1\,GHz \\
Cortex-M23 / M33 & ARMv8-M & + TrustZone security extension (M33 also DSP and FPU) \\
Cortex-M52 / M55 / M85 & ARMv8.1-M & + Helium vector extension (MVE) for signal processing and machine learning \\
\hline
\end{tabular}}
\end{center}
\bi
	\ite common to all: NVIC interrupt controller, SysTick timer, vector table, debug via SWD
	\ite upward compatible: code for the M0+ runs on all others
	\ite most used today: M0+ (cheap), M4 (DSP + FPU), M33 (security); M55/M85 for edge AI
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf Cortex-M instruction set particularities}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\bi
	\ite \textbf{Thumb-2}: 16 and 32 bit instructions mixed freely -- good code density at almost full ARM performance
	\ite conditional execution with \texttt{IT} blocks (``if-then''): up to 4 instructions without a branch
	\ite load/store multiple (\texttt{push \{r4-r7, lr\}}, \texttt{ldm}, \texttt{stm}), compare-and-branch-if-zero (\texttt{cbz})
	\ite \textbf{DSP extension} (M4, M7, M33): single-cycle MAC (\texttt{smlabb}), SIMD on 2 $\times$ 16 or 4 $\times$ 8 bit (\texttt{sadd16}, \texttt{smlad}), saturation (\texttt{ssat}, \texttt{qadd})
	\ite \textbf{ARMv8.1-M}: low-overhead loops (\texttt{dls}/\texttt{le}), Helium vectors on 128 bit registers (8 $\times$ 16 bit, 4 $\times$ float), pointer authentication against return-oriented programming (PACBTI, M85)
	\ite exception entry in hardware: \texttt{r0}--\texttt{r3}, \texttt{r12}, \texttt{lr}, \texttt{pc}, \texttt{xpsr} are stacked automatically $\Rightarrow$ an interrupt handler is a normal C function
	\ite calling convention (AAPCS): arguments in \texttt{r0}--\texttt{r3}, return value in \texttt{r0}, \texttt{r4}--\texttt{r11} must be preserved
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf TrustZone for Cortex-M (ARMv8-M)}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\bi
	\ite the processor runs in a \textbf{secure} or a \textbf{non-secure} state; memory and peripherals are assigned to one of the states
	\bi
		\ite SAU/IDAU (security attribution unit) defines the regions; the switch to the secure state is only possible through defined entry points (\texttt{sg} instruction)
	\ei
	\ite the secure side holds keys, the secure boot loader, crypto and firmware update -- the application cannot read them even if it is compromised
	\ite reference software: Trusted Firmware-M (TF-M), certification after PSA Certified
	\ite important for the EU Cyber Resilience Act (CRA): products with digital elements must be secure by design and receive security updates
	\ite available in Cortex-M23, M33, M35P, M52, M55, M85 (e.g.\ RP2350, STM32U5, nRF54L, LPC55)
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf RISC-V}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\bi
	\ite open instruction set architecture (since 2010, UC Berkeley; today RISC-V International) -- no license fees
	\ite modular: a small base set plus standard extensions
	\bi
		\ite \textbf{RV32I}: 32 bit integer base, 32 registers (\texttt{x0} is always 0); \textbf{RV32E}: only 16 registers for the smallest cores
		\ite \textbf{M} multiplication/division, \textbf{A} atomic operations, \textbf{F}/\textbf{D} floating point, \textbf{C} compressed 16 bit instructions, \textbf{V} vectors, \textbf{B} bit manipulation
		\ite \textbf{Zc*}: further compressed instructions for code size (e.g.\ \texttt{cm.push}/\texttt{cm.pop})
		\ite ESP32-C3 = RV32IMC; Hazard3 in the RP2350 = RV32IMAC + bit manipulation + Zc*
	\ei
	\ite \textbf{profiles} bundle extensions: RVA23 for application processors (Linux distributions target it), smaller profiles for MCUs
	\ite \textbf{privilege modes}: M (machine), S (supervisor, for Linux), U (user); embedded: M + U with PMP (physical memory protection) as MPU
	\ite \textbf{custom instructions} are allowed and documented: e.g.\ DSP or AI instructions for a single application
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf RISC-V: chances and challenges}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\bi
	\ite \textbf{chances}
	\bi
		\ite no license fee and no dependence on one company -- strategically important (EU, China, India)
		\ite many implementations: commercial IP (SiFive, Andes, Codasip, Nuclei), vendor cores (Espressif, WCH, Nordic), open source (Hazard3, Ibex in OpenTitan, CV32E40P from OpenHW, VexRiscv)
		\ite the same compilers (GCC, LLVM), the same Rust targets, Zephyr and FreeRTOS ports as for ARM
		\ite popular as small coprocessor next to an ARM core: nRF54 (VPR), ESP32-C6 (LP core), many SoCs for management and security tasks
	\ei
	\ite \textbf{challenges}
	\bi
		\ite fragmentation: which extensions, which custom instructions, which interrupt controller (CLINT, CLIC, PLIC or vendor-specific)?
		\ite the ecosystem of debug probes, trace and certified safety tools is younger than for ARM
		\ite verification: every vendor must prove that its core implements the ISA correctly
	\ei
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf Xtensa and other architectures}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\bi
	\ite \textbf{Xtensa} (Cadence): configurable cores; the customer chooses options and adds own instructions
	\bi
		\ite ESP32 (LX6), ESP32-S3 (LX7 with vector instructions for AI); Espressif's newer chips use RISC-V
		\ite \textbf{register windows}: \texttt{entry}/\texttt{retw} rotate a window of 16 out of 64 registers -- fewer pushes and pops, but a window overflow causes an exception
		\ite zero-overhead \texttt{loop}, MAC16 unit; also used in many audio DSPs (HiFi)
	\ei
	\ite \textbf{Infineon TriCore} (AURIX): RISC, MCU and DSP in one, up to 6 cores, lock-step, hardware security module -- leader in engine and chassis control units
	\ite \textbf{Renesas RH850}: automotive MCUs; \textbf{Renesas RX}: 32 bit CISC with variable instruction length (1--8 bytes)
	\ite \textbf{TI C2000}: DSP core C28x plus real-time control accelerators (CLA) for motor control and power electronics
	\ite \textbf{Synopsys ARC}: configurable cores, often hidden inside SoCs (sensor hubs, SSD controllers)
	\ite \textbf{PIC}, \textbf{8051}, \textbf{MSP430}: 8 and 16 bit, still common in cost-sensitive mass products
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf Assembly: the same C function on four processors}]~\\[-\lstskip]
\begin{lstlisting}[language=C]
uint32_t sum(const uint16_t *a, int n) {
    uint32_t s = 0;
    for (int i = 0; i < n; i++) {
        s += a[i];
    }
    return s;
}
\end{lstlisting}
\begin{center}
\resizebox{0.85\textwidth}{!}{%
\begin{tabular}{|l|c|c|l|}
\hline
\textbf{processor} & \textbf{instructions in the loop} & \textbf{code size} & \textbf{reason} \\
\hline
AVR (8 bit) & 12 & 40 bytes & 32 bit sum and 16 bit index need several 8 bit operations \\
Cortex-M0+ & 7 & 24 bytes & 16 bit Thumb instructions \\
Cortex-M4 & 6 & 24 bytes & load with scaled index: \texttt{ldrh r4, [r0, r3, lsl \#1]} \\
RISC-V RV32IMC & 7 & 28 bytes & mix of 16 and 32 bit instructions \\
\hline
\end{tabular}}
\end{center}
\bi
	\ite compiled with \texttt{-Os} (optimise for size); listings and script in \texttt{examples/\-processors},\\ or try it on \url{https://godbolt.org}
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf The loop in assembly: AVR and Cortex-M0+}]~\\[-\lstskip]
\begin{lstlisting}[language={}]
; AVR: s in r22..r25 (4 x 8 bit), pointer in Z (r31:r30), i in r19:r18
.L3:  ld   r26,Z+            ; low byte of a[i], Z++
      ld   r27,Z+            ; high byte of a[i], Z++
      add  r22,r26           ; s += a[i], byte by byte with carry
      adc  r23,r27
      adc  r24,__zero_reg__
      adc  r25,__zero_reg__
      subi r18,-1            ; i++ (16 bit)
      sbci r19,-1
.L2:  cp   r18,r20           ; i < n ?
      cpc  r19,r21
      brlt .L3
\end{lstlisting}
\begin{lstlisting}[language={}]
@ Cortex-M0+: a in r0, n in r1, i in r3, s in r2
.L3:  lsls  r4, r3, #1       @ byte offset = i * 2
      ldrh  r4, [r0, r4]     @ load halfword a[i]
      adds  r3, r3, #1       @ i++
      adds  r2, r2, r4       @ s += a[i]
      b     .L2
.L2:  cmp   r3, r1           @ i < n ?
      blt   .L3
\end{lstlisting}
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf The loop in assembly: RISC-V}]~\\[-\lstskip]
\begin{lstlisting}[language={}]
# RISC-V: a in a4 (copied from a0), n in a1, i in a5, s in a0 (= return value)
.L2:  blt   a5,a1,.L3        # i < n ? compare and branch in one instruction
      ret
.L3:  slli  a3,a5,1          # byte offset = i * 2
      add   a3,a4,a3         # address = a + offset
      lhu   a3,0(a3)         # load halfword unsigned
      addi  a5,a5,1          # i++
      add   a0,a0,a3         # s += a[i]
      j     .L2
\end{lstlisting}
\bi
	\ite RISC-V has no status flags: compare and branch are one instruction (\texttt{blt})
	\ite only one addressing mode (register + 12 bit offset) -- the address is computed with normal instructions
	\ite register names of the calling convention: \texttt{a0}--\texttt{a7} arguments and return values, \texttt{s0}--\texttt{s11} preserved, \texttt{sp} stack pointer, \texttt{ra} return address, \texttt{zero} always 0
	\ite more about assembly programming in chapter Programming
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf A DSP kernel: dot product in fixed point}]~\\[-\lstskip]
\begin{lstlisting}[language=C]
int32_t dot_q15(const int16_t *a, const int16_t *b, int n) {
    int32_t s = 0;
    for (int i = 0; i < n; i++)
        s += (int32_t)a[i] * b[i];
    return s;
}
\end{lstlisting}
\begin{center}
\resizebox{0.95\textwidth}{!}{%
\begin{tabular}{|l|c|l|}
\hline
\textbf{processor} & \textbf{instr.\ in loop} & \textbf{what the compiler uses (\texttt{-O2})} \\
\hline
Cortex-M0+ & 7 & \texttt{ldrsh}, \texttt{muls}, \texttt{adds}, compare and branch \\
RISC-V RV32IMC & 7 & \texttt{lh}, \texttt{mul}, \texttt{add} -- no multiply-accumulate in the base ISA \\
Cortex-M4 / M33 & 5 & \texttt{ldrh} with pointer increment, \texttt{smlabb}: 16 $\times$ 16 + 32 bit in one instruction \\
Cortex-M55 & 4 & as M4, plus low-overhead loop \texttt{dls}/\texttt{le}: no compare, no branch \\
Xtensa LX7 (ESP32-S3) & 5 & zero-overhead \texttt{loop}, MAC16 \texttt{mula.aa.ll} into the accumulator \texttt{ACCLO} \\
\hline
\end{tabular}}
\end{center}
\bi
	\ite DSP features = fewer instructions \textbf{and} no branch penalty per iteration
	\ite the compiler finds MAC and loop instructions by itself; SIMD (\texttt{smlad}: two MACs at once) usually needs intrinsics or CMSIS-DSP
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf The DSP loop in assembly: Cortex-M4 and Xtensa}]~\\[-\lstskip]
\begin{lstlisting}[language={}]
@ Cortex-M4: r3 -> a, r1 -> b, r2 = end of a, s in r0
.L3:  ldrh    lr, [r3, #2]!     @ load a[i], pointer += 2 (pre-increment)
      ldrh    ip, [r1, #2]!     @ load b[i], pointer += 2
      cmp     r3, r2            @ end reached?
      smlabb  r0, lr, ip, r0    @ s += a[i] * b[i] (bottom halves, signed)
      bne     .L3
\end{lstlisting}
\begin{lstlisting}[language={}]
# Xtensa LX7: a8 = iteration count, a9 -> a, a3 -> b
      wsr     a10, ACCLO        # accumulator = 0
      loop    a8, .L3_LEND      # hardware loop: repeat a8 times
.L3:  l16ui   a11, a9, 0        # load a[i]
      l16ui   a10, a3, 0        # load b[i]
      addi.n  a9, a9, 2
      mula.aa.ll a11, a10       # ACC += a[i] * b[i]
      addi.n  a3, a3, 2
.L3_LEND:
      rsr     a2, ACCLO         # result from the accumulator
\end{lstlisting}
\bi
	\ite the compiler schedules \texttt{cmp} before \texttt{smlabb} so that the flags are ready for \texttt{bne} without a stall
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf Floating point: software, FPU and vector unit}]~\\[-\lstskip]
\begin{lstlisting}[language={}]
@ Cortex-M0+ (no FPU): every operation is a library call of 30-100 cycles
      bl    __aeabi_fmul        @ a[i] * b[i]
      bl    __aeabi_fadd        @ s + product
@ Cortex-M4F: fused multiply-add in the FPU, 3 cycles
      vfma.f32  s0, s14, s15    @ s0 += s14 * s15
@ Cortex-M55 with -ffast-math: Helium, 4 floats per instruction
.L14: vldrw.32  q3, [ip], #16   @ load a[i..i+3]
      vldrw.32  q1, [r3], #16   @ load b[i..i+3]
      vmul.f32  q3, q3, q1
      vadd.f32  q2, q2, q3      @ 4 partial sums
      le        lr, .L14        @ low-overhead loop end
\end{lstlisting}
\bi
	\ite without \texttt{-ffast-math} the M55 multiplies vectors, but adds the products one by one: float addition is not associative, and C requires the order of the source code
	\ite ESP32-C3 (RV32IMC) calls \texttt{\_\_mulsf3}/\texttt{\_\_addsf3}; the ESP32-S3 has an FPU (\texttt{madd.s}); RP2040 has fast float routines in its boot ROM
\ei
\os{\newpage}
\end{description}

\section{Inside a Microcontroller}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\nsl{The processor core is only a small part of a microcontroller. Around it
are memories of different kinds, a bus system that connects the core with
the peripherals, a clock system and a power management unit. How these parts
work determines how fast a program really runs, how exactly its timing can
be predicted and how long a battery lasts.}

\begin{description}
\item[{\rot\bf Block diagram of a microcontroller}]~\\[-\lblskip]

\bi
	\ite the bus matrix lets the CPU and the DMA access different memories at the same time
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf Memories}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\bi
	\ite \textbf{flash}: non-volatile program memory; erased in blocks, limited number of write cycles (typically 10\,000--100\,000)
	\bi
		\ite at high clock rates flash is too slow $\Rightarrow$ wait states, prefetch buffers or a cache
		\ite RP2040 and ESP32 execute code directly from an external QSPI flash through a cache (``execute in place'', XIP) -- a cache miss costs many cycles
	\ei
	\ite \textbf{SRAM}: fast, volatile working memory for data, stack and time-critical code
	\ite \textbf{EEPROM} or flash emulation: small, byte-wise changeable non-volatile data (settings, calibration)
	\ite new non-volatile memories in recent MCUs: MRAM, RRAM, FRAM -- byte-writable, fast, many write cycles (e.g.\ in 22\,nm and smaller processes, where embedded flash is hard to build)
	\ite \textbf{MPU} (memory protection unit): protects memory regions (e.g.\ catches stack overflows); an MMU with virtual memory exists only on application processors
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf Memory map of the RP2040}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\begin{center}
\resizebox{0.9\textwidth}{!}{%
\begin{tabular}{|l|l|}
\hline
\textbf{address} & \textbf{content} \\
\hline
0x0000\,0000 & boot ROM (16\,KB) \\
0x1000\,0000 & external flash via XIP cache (code, constants) \\
0x2000\,0000 & SRAM (264\,KB: data, stack, heap) \\
0x4000\,0000 & peripherals on the APB bus (UART, SPI, I2C, ADC, PWM, timer, \ldots) \\
0x5000\,0000 & peripherals on the AHB bus (DMA, USB, PIO) \\
0xd000\,0000 & SIO: single-cycle I/O (GPIO, hardware divider, spinlocks) \\
0xe000\,0000 & Cortex-M0+ internal registers (NVIC, SysTick) \\
\hline
\end{tabular}}
\end{center}
\bi
	\ite one 32 bit address space for everything: memory-mapped I/O (see chapter Peripherals and Interfaces)
	\ite the linker places code and data into these regions according to the linker script (\texttt{memory.x} in our Rust examples)
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf Interrupts and exceptions}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\bi
	\ite the \textbf{vector table} at the start of the program contains the initial stack pointer and the addresses of all handlers (reset, faults, interrupts)
	\ite Cortex-M: the hardware saves 8 registers on the stack before the handler starts, so a handler is an ordinary C or Rust function
	\ite interrupt latency: 15 clock cycles on the Cortex-M0+, 12 on the Cortex-M3/M4/M33
	\ite \textbf{NVIC}: priorities, nesting, and \textbf{tail chaining} -- a pending interrupt starts directly after the current one without restoring and saving the registers again
	\ite \textbf{faults} (HardFault, MemManage, BusFault, UsageFault): invalid memory accesses, undefined instructions, division by zero -- the fault handler is the place to log the error and reset
	\ite RISC-V: similar concepts, but the interrupt controller depends on the implementation (CLINT, CLIC, PLIC or vendor-specific); with CLIC, vectored and nested interrupts similar to the NVIC
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf Clock and power}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\bi
	\ite clock sources: internal RC oscillator (fast start, inaccurate), external crystal (accurate), PLL to multiply the frequency
	\ite dynamic power $P \approx C \cdot U^2 \cdot f$: lower voltage and frequency save energy
	\ite peripherals that are not used should have their clock switched off (clock gating)
	\ite sleep modes: from ``CPU stopped, peripherals running'' to ``everything off except a wake-up timer''
	\bi
		\ite ESP32-C3 deep sleep: about 5\,$\mu$A; Wi-Fi transmission: well above 100\,mA
		\ite a battery device spends most of its time sleeping: average current = sum of (current $\times$ time share)
	\ei
	\ite example: 2000\,mAh battery, 10\,$\mu$A sleep and 1 second at 100\,mA every 10 minutes $\Rightarrow$ average $\approx 0.18$\,mA $\Rightarrow$ about 15 months
	\ite ``race to sleep'': a faster core that finishes earlier and sleeps longer often needs less energy than a slow one
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf Arithmetic: integer, fixed point, floating point}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\bi
	\ite without FPU (AVR, Cortex-M0+, ESP32-C3), floating point is done in software: tens to hundreds of cycles per operation
	\ite \textbf{fixed point}: integers with an implied scale, e.g.\ Q15 = value $\times 2^{15}$ in 16 bit
	\bi
		\ite fast on every processor, but the programmer must watch range and rounding
	\ei
	\ite FPU (Cortex-M4F, M7, M33, ESP32-S3): single precision float in one or a few cycles -- \texttt{double} is still done in software if the FPU is single precision only
	\ite \textbf{DSP features}: multiply-accumulate (MAC) in one cycle, saturating arithmetic, SIMD (two 16 bit or four 8 bit operations in one 32 bit instruction)
	\ite dedicated DSPs add zero-overhead loops, circular addressing and several MAC units (VLIW) -- today often replaced by Cortex-M4/M7 or vector extensions
	\ite neural networks run in 8 bit integers (INT8 quantisation): SIMD and vector units, or a separate NPU
\ei
\os{\newpage}
\end{description}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Manufacturers and their Product Lines}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\nsl{Processor cores and chips usually come from different companies. IP
vendors such as Arm, SiFive, Andes or Cadence design cores and license them.
Semiconductor manufacturers combine licensed or own cores with memories,
peripherals and analog circuits and sell the chips. The market has been
consolidated by acquisitions: Microchip bought Atmel (2016), NXP merged with
Freescale (2015), Infineon bought Cypress (2020), Renesas bought Dialog
(2021). Because the cores are similar, the manufacturers differ mainly in
their peripherals, in their focus markets, in their software tools and in
how long they deliver a chip.}

\begin{description}
\item[{\rot\bf Who makes what}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\bi
	\ite \textbf{IP vendors} design cores and license them, they sell no chips
	\bi
		\ite Arm (Cortex-M/R/A): license fee plus royalty per chip
		\ite RISC-V IP: SiFive, Andes, Codasip, Nuclei; free cores from OpenHW, lowRISC, Raspberry Pi (Hazard3)
		\ite Cadence (Xtensa), Synopsys (ARC), CEVA (DSPs)
	\ei
	\ite \textbf{semiconductor manufacturers} build MCUs and SoCs from licensed or own cores
	\ite \textbf{foundries} manufacture the chips for companies without own fabs (TSMC, Samsung, GlobalFoundries, UMC)
	\bi
		\ite MCUs mostly in mature processes (90--22\,nm) with embedded flash; application processors in 16--3\,nm
		\ite chip shortage 2021--2023: delivery times of more than a year $\Rightarrow$ second source and availability became selection criteria
	\ei
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf Semiconductor manufacturers (1): the broad-line vendors}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\begin{center}
{\footnotesize
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{|>{\raggedright}p{24mm}|L|L|>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{27mm}|}
\hline
\textbf{vendor} & \textbf{families} & \textbf{particularities} & \textbf{tools} \\
\hline
STMicro\-electronics & STM32 (Cortex-M0+ \ldots M55), STM32MP (Cortex-A) & broadest Cortex-M portfolio, pin-compatible families, large community, cheap Nucleo boards & STM32CubeMX/IDE, HAL/LL drivers \\

\section{Application Processors and Heterogeneous SoCs}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\nsl{When an application needs a graphical user interface, a camera, a lot
of memory or complex network software, a microcontroller is no longer enough.
Application processors run an operating system with virtual memory such as
Linux. Most of them are heterogeneous systems on chip: next to the
application cores they contain microcontroller cores for real-time tasks,
graphics and video units and, more and more, accelerators for neural
networks.}

\begin{description}
\item[{\rot\bf Application processors}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\bi
	\ite ARM Cortex-A (and RISC-V application cores) with
	\bi
		\ite \textbf{MMU}: virtual memory, every process has its own protected address space -- required by Linux
		\ite several cores, GHz clock rates, multi-level caches (L1 per core, shared L2/L3), out-of-order execution in larger cores
		\ite external DRAM (hundreds of MB to several GB), storage on eMMC, SD card or NVMe
	\ei
	\ite examples: Raspberry Pi 5 (4 $\times$ Cortex-A76, 2.4\,GHz), NXP i.MX\,93/95, TI AM62x, STM32MP2, Rockchip RK3588
	\ite RISC-V application cores are arriving: SiFive, T-Head, SpacemiT; Linux distributions target the RVA23 profile
	\ite often with GPU, video codecs, image signal processor and neural network accelerators (NPU)
	\ite non-deterministic timing (caches, DRAM, branch prediction, OS) $\Rightarrow$ hard real time on a separate core or MCU
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf Heterogeneous SoC: Linux and real time on one chip}]~\\[-\lblskip]

\bi
	\ite neural network inference = mostly multiply-accumulate on INT8 $\Rightarrow$ dedicated NPUs with hundreds to thousands of MAC units
	\ite key figure: \textbf{TOPS} (tera operations per second) and TOPS/W -- a peak value, real models reach only a part of it
	\ite microcontroller class (mW, kB--MB models): Arm Ethos-U55/U65/U85 next to Cortex-M55/M85 (e.g.\ Alif Ensemble, Infineon PSoC Edge), STM32N6 (Neural-ART, about 0.6 TOPS), NXP MCX N (eIQ Neutron)

	\ite software: model trained in PyTorch or TensorFlow, quantised to INT8, compiled for the target (LiteRT for Microcontrollers, CMSIS-NN, vendor tools such as STM32Cube.AI or eIQ)
	\ite more about AI in embedded systems in the introduction chapter

\section{Excursion: Microcontroller or FPGA?}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\nsl{A processor executes instructions one after the other; even a
superscalar core does only a few things at the same time. A field
programmable gate array (FPGA) contains no fixed processor, but thousands of
small configurable logic blocks and a configurable network between them. The
algorithm is turned into a circuit, and all parts of this circuit work in
parallel in every clock cycle. This section compares the two approaches and
shows where they meet.}

\begin{description}
\item[{\rot\bf What is inside an FPGA}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\begin{figure}[!hbtp]
\begin{center}
\resizebox{0.75\textwidth}{!}{%
\begin{tikzpicture}[font=\sffamily\small,>=Stealth,
	b/.style={draw,thick,minimum height=12mm,align=center}]
	\foreach \i in {0,...,3} \draw[->,thick] (-1.2,1.05-\i*0.3) -- (0,1.05-\i*0.3);
	\node[left,font=\sffamily\scriptsize] at (-1.2,0.6) {inputs};
	\node[b,fill=rwucyan!20,minimum width=22mm,minimum height=16mm] (lut) at (1.1,0.6) {LUT\\(16 $\times$ 1 bit\\memory)};
	\node[b,fill=rwuviolet!20,minimum width=14mm] (ff) at (4,0.9) {D flip-\\flop};
	\node[draw,thick,trapezium,trapezium angle=70,shape border rotate=270,minimum width=6mm,minimum height=14mm,fill=gray!10] (mux) at (6,0.6) {};
	\draw[->,thick] (lut.east) -- ++(0.5,0) |- (ff.west);
	\draw[->,thick] (lut.east) -- ++(0.5,0) |- ([yshift=-3mm]mux.west);
	\draw[->,thick] (ff.east) -- ([yshift=3mm]mux.west);
	\draw[->,thick] (mux.east) -- ++(1,0) node[right,font=\sffamily\scriptsize]{to routing};
	\node[font=\sffamily\scriptsize] at (4,-0.3) {clock};
	\draw[->,thick] (4,-0.15) -- (ff.south);
\end{tikzpicture}}
\caption{Basic logic element: look-up table, flip-flop, multiplexer}
\end{center}
\end{figure}
\bi
	\ite a 4- or 6-input \textbf{look-up table} (LUT) realises any logic function of its inputs; thousands to millions of them
	\ite programmable routing, \textbf{block RAM}, \textbf{DSP slices} (hardware multipliers), clock managers (PLL), fast transceivers
	\ite the configuration is loaded from flash at power-up (SRAM-based FPGAs) or stored on chip (flash-based, e.g.\ Microchip PolarFire)
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf Sequential versus parallel}]~\\[-\lstskip]
\begin{lstlisting}[language=C]
// microcontroller: the CPU does one thing after the other
while (1) {
    cnt++;
    gpio_put(LED, (cnt >> 23) & 1);
}
\end{lstlisting}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Verilog]
// FPGA: describes a circuit - a 24 bit counter register and a wire
module blink(input clk, output led);
  reg [23:0] cnt = 0;
  always @(posedge clk) cnt <= cnt + 1;
  assign led = cnt[23];
endmodule
\end{lstlisting}
\bi
	\ite 100 such counters on the MCU: 100 times slower; on the FPGA: 100 copies of the circuit, all at full speed
	\ite the Verilog (or VHDL) code is synthesised into LUTs and flip-flops -- it is a hardware description, not a program
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf Microcontroller and FPGA compared}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\begin{center}
{\footnotesize
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{|l|L|L|}
\hline
 & \textbf{microcontroller} & \textbf{FPGA} \\
\hline
execution & sequential instructions & parallel circuits \\
timing & $\mu$s, interrupt latency and jitter & ns, cycle-exact and deterministic \\
throughput & limited by the clock and the core & scales with the chip size (parallelism) \\
languages & C, C++, Rust, MicroPython & VHDL, Verilog, SystemVerilog; HLS from C++; Chisel, Amaranth \\
development time & short; debugging with breakpoints & long; simulation, timing closure, synthesis takes minutes to hours \\
power & $\mu$W in sleep & static power, usually mW to W; no deep sleep (exceptions: iCE40, Lattice CrossLink) \\
price & from 0.10\,\texteuro{} & from a few euros; large devices hundreds to thousands of euros \\
change after delivery & firmware update & reconfiguration (also a firmware update) \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}}
\end{center}
\bi
	\ite FPGA where the MCU is too slow or too irregular: image processing at line rate, high-speed ADC interfaces, many parallel PWM or encoder channels, protocol bridges, real-time Ethernet (TSN, EtherCAT)
	\ite and as a prototype for an ASIC
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf Where microcontroller and FPGA meet}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\bi
	\ite \textbf{programmable I/O in MCUs}: RP2040/RP2350 PIO -- small state machines that bit-bang protocols (VGA, WS2812, DShot) cycle-exactly; also configurable logic cells (CCL in AVR, CLB in some PIC and TI C2000)
	\ite \textbf{soft cores}: a processor built from FPGA logic
	\bi
		\ite AMD MicroBlaze V and Altera Nios V -- both RISC-V since 2023; open source: VexRiscv, NEORV32, PicoRV32
	\ei
	\ite \textbf{SoC-FPGAs}: hard processor cores plus FPGA fabric on one chip
	\bi
		\ite AMD Zynq-7000 (2 $\times$ Cortex-A9), Zynq UltraScale+ MPSoC (Cortex-A53 + R5F), Versal (with AI engines)
		\ite Altera Agilex 5 and Cyclone V SoC, Microchip PolarFire SoC (5 RISC-V cores, Linux-capable)
	\ei
	\ite \textbf{small FPGAs with open toolchains}: Lattice iCE40/ECP5 and Gowin with Yosys + nextpnr -- easy start for students
	\ite rule of thumb: start with an MCU; use an FPGA (or PIO) only for the part that the MCU cannot do in time
\ei
\os{\newpage}
\end{description}

\section{Choosing a Processor}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\nsl{The last part of this chapter summarises the selection criteria in a
checklist and gives exercises that use the examples of this chapter.}

\begin{description}
\item[{\rot\bf Choosing a processor: checklist}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\bi
	\ite \textbf{function}: required peripherals (number of UARTs, ADC resolution, CAN, USB, Ethernet, radio)
	\ite \textbf{performance}: computing power, FPU or DSP needed? Real-time requirements, interrupt latency, determinism?
	\ite \textbf{memory}: flash and RAM with reserve for later updates (rule of thumb: start with 50\,\% usage at most)
	\ite \textbf{energy}: battery or mains? Sleep current, wake-up time
	\ite \textbf{environment}: temperature range, automotive or industrial qualification, safety certification
	\ite \textbf{security}: secure boot, TrustZone, crypto accelerators, update capability (Cyber Resilience Act)
	\ite \textbf{software}: compilers, SDK, RTOS and Rust support, example code, community
	\ite \textbf{business}: price at the planned quantity, availability for 10+ years, second source
	\ite in practice the software ecosystem and the availability decide more often than the core itself
\ei
\os{\newpage}

\item[{\rot\bf Exercises}]~\\[-\lblskip]
\bi
	\ite Compile \texttt{sum.c} on \url{https://godbolt.org} for the Cortex-M0+ with \texttt{-O0}, \texttt{-Os} and \texttt{-O2}. Compare the number of instructions in the loop.
	\ite Change the type of \texttt{s} to \texttt{uint16\_t}. Which processor profits most, and why?
	\ite Compile \texttt{dot.c} for the Cortex-M55 with and without \texttt{-ffast-math}. Why does the result of \texttt{dot\_f32} possibly change in the last bits?
	\ite Rewrite \texttt{dot\_q15} with the CMSIS intrinsic \texttt{\_\_SMLAD} so that two products are computed per instruction on the Cortex-M4. What must be true for the arrays?
	\ite A data cache has 32\,KB, 4 ways and 64 byte lines. How many sets does it have, and how many bits are used for tag, index and offset of a 32 bit address?
	\ite Estimate the battery lifetime of a sensor that wakes up every minute for 50\,ms at 20\,mA and sleeps at 5\,$\mu$A (battery 1000\,mAh).
	\ite Choose a processor (MCU, MPU or FPGA) for: (a) a battery-powered weather station with Wi-Fi, (b) a 3-phase motor inverter with 20\,kHz PWM, (c) a camera with person detection, (d) a 64-channel ultrasound front end sampling at 40\,MHz. Justify your choice with the checklist.
\ei
\end{description}
